\section{A first image}\label{sec:first-image}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{In plain words.} Nothing absorbs the glow, so a thermal gravitational
picture is a sum: each pixel adds up the glow of everything along its line of
sight. Here a camera on the Earth's night side looks down at a planet of five
shells, from a crust at $700\,\mathrm{K}$ to an inner core at
$5500\,\mathrm{K}$, each given the most friction that its stiffness allows;
every brightness of the Earth is an upper bound.

The limit is the count of
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton}{gravitons}. A square metre
receives at most one from the whole Earth every twenty hours, about 36,000 in
eighty years, enough for a picture of some fourteen elements. Because the planet
is round, a steeper line of sight reaches one layer deeper, so the layers can be
peeled off one at a time from the brightness at each angle (the inversion of an
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_transform}{Abel transform}), and one
viewpoint is enough. Each boundary puts a kink in the brightness at an angle set
by its radius alone, and the edge of the core is found to within
$29\,\mathrm{km}$. If the friction is flat across the band, the counts also give
each layer's temperature and
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terahertz_radiation}{terahertz} friction,
which no seismometer reaches. These pictures show what is there, not what could
be recorded: a cubic metre of any detector made of matter would wait
$2\times10^{29}$ years for its first count.
\end{quote}

Because nothing absorbs or scatters, a thermal gravitational image is a line
integral. Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff} over frequency for constant
$\eta$ gives the power emitted per unit volume and solid angle,
$j_E = \alpha\sigma T^4/\pi$, where $\sigma$ is the Stefan--Boltzmann constant.
Dividing by the mean energy of a thermal quantum, $2.70\,\kB T$, gives a
number emissivity $j_N$, and the brightness of the pixel that looks along the
unit vector $\hat n$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:column}
N(\hat n) = \int_0^\infty j_N\bigl(s\,\hat n\bigr)\,\dd s ,
\end{equation}
in gravitons per second, per square metre of aperture, per steradian.

Figure~\ref{fig:sun-at-midnight} evaluates Eq.~\eqref{eq:column} for an
observer on the night side of the Earth, at latitude $40^\circ$ at local
midnight. The Earth is modelled as five uniform shells with rounded textbook
radii and temperatures, from a crust at $700\,\mathrm{K}$ to an inner core at
$5500\,\mathrm{K}$. Looking straight down, the line of sight runs through
$12{,}700\,\mathrm{km}$ of rock and iron, and the expected brightness is at
most $1.2\times10^{-5}$ gravitons per second per square metre per steradian,
each shell being given the most that its stiffness allows (below).
Lines of sight that miss the core are fainter (Fig.~\ref{fig:earth-glow}). The
Sun radiates of order $10^{8}\,\mathrm{W}$ in gravitational waves
\cite{GarciaCelyRingwald2025,Steane2024,Weinberg1965}, about
$4\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ at the Earth: roughly one graviton per
square metre per second, arriving from a patch of sky a tenth of a degree
across. Its surface brightness, about $4\times10^{5}$ gravitons per second per
square metre per steradian at the centre of the core in our model, is more
than $10^{10}$ times that of the Earth's interior, so the Sun dominates the thermal
gravitational sky by day and by night.

The Moon, in light the brightest thing in the night sky, is faint in thermal
gravitational radiation: its centre is about eighty
times fainter than the ground underfoot, because its interior is cooler,
$1200$--$1700\,\mathrm{K}$ in our model, and a line of sight through it is a
quarter as long.

The shells' radii and densities are rounded from the Preliminary Reference
Earth Model \cite{DziewonskiAnderson1981}. Two assumptions carry most of the
uncertainty. The Earth's internal friction
at thermal frequencies is not known. Each shell is therefore given the
most that its stiffness allows, Eq.~\eqref{eq:glow-bound}, between $1.6$ and
$3.4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ (see \emph{What the Earth's materials
allow}, below), so that every brightness of the Earth in this section is an
upper bound. The Sun's
emission is spread uniformly over the inner fifth of its radius, where the
true profile is peaked toward the centre.

\paragraph{The graviton budget.} The image is an expectation value. Figure~\ref{fig:budget}
sets the numbers of gravitons emitted side by side, and Table~\ref{tab:budget}
the numbers a camera would receive in each of the scenes of this paper.
The Sun delivers about one graviton per square metre per second. The whole
Earth below delivers at most one every twenty hours,
$3.6\times10^{4}$ per square metre in eighty years. That is a coarse picture: picking out features of
ten per cent contrast at five standard deviations takes about $2500$ counts
each, so a lifetime's exposure resolves about fourteen picture
elements. Everyday objects in a room take millions of years
for a single graviton. These counts assume no other background. If the
primordial glow of Section~\ref{sec:vistas} exists, its
$3.6\times10^{14}$ gravitons per second per square metre per steradian dwarf
every one of them; but it is cold, and an ideal filter passing only frequencies
above two terahertz leaves $4\times10^{-43}$ of it, fainter than anything in
this paper, while keeping $98\%$ of the gravitons from a body at room
temperature, and more from hotter ones (with the
friction taken as constant across the band). Its fluctuations, not its known
mean, are what such a filter must remove.

\paragraph{Recovering the layers.} A camera on the surface of a round planet
needs no other viewpoint to see inside it. The line of sight at angle $\theta$
from straight down passes the centre at the distance $b = R\sin\theta$ and
crosses the planet along the whole chord at that distance, so for an emissivity
that depends only on the radius, Eq.~\eqref{eq:column} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abel}
N(b) = 2\int_b^R \frac{j_N(r)\,r\,\dd r}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}} ,
\end{equation}
the Abel transform of the profile $j_N(r)$, which can be inverted:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abel-inverse}
j_N(r) = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_r^R \frac{\dd N}{\dd b}\,\frac{\dd b}{\sqrt{b^2 - r^2}} .
\end{equation}
The planet's symmetry supplies the other viewpoints that tomography would need.
For shells of uniform emissivity the inversion is a peeling. The line of sight
that grazes the bottom of a shell crosses only that shell and those above it, so
the crust's emissivity is read first and each deeper one in turn, as in the
onion-peeling reconstructions of flames and plasmas \cite{Dasch1992}. Where the
emissivity jumps, at a boundary of radius $r$, $\dd N/\dd b$ diverges as
$(r - b)^{-1/2}$: the brightness has a kink at $\sin\theta = r/R$, and these
kinks are the steps of Fig.~\ref{fig:earth-glow}. Their places are geometry,
the radii of the boundaries; the friction, the temperatures and the exposure set
only their heights.

The heights measure a product. From the definitions above,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jN-friction}
j_N = \frac{16\,G\,\eta\,\sigma T^3}{2.70\,\kB\,c^3} ,
\end{equation}
so counted gravitons give $\eta T^3$ in each shell, and their energies give
$\eta T^4$. If the friction is flat across the thermal band, the two together
give each shell's temperature, from the mean energy $2.70\,\kB T$ of the
gravitons it sends, and then its friction. The camera is then a thermometer for
the deep Earth and a viscometer for its friction at terahertz frequencies, which
no seismometer reaches. If the friction varies across the band, each shell's
spectrum is $\alpha(\omega)$ times a Planck spectrum, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff},
and the temperature trades off against the shape of $\alpha$.

Figure~\ref{fig:earth-inverted} shows what a lifetime gives: eighty years of
counts on a square metre that collects from every direction (about
$3.6\times10^4$ gravitons with the shells at their bounds, the Sun's path
across the ground masked), fitted by maximum likelihood with five shells, radii
and emissivities together. In rings of one degree such a fit is
expected, from the Fisher information of the model, to place the boundary of
the core to within $29\,\mathrm{km}$ and the top of the lower mantle to within
$5\,\mathrm{km}$ (one standard deviation), and to measure the
emissivity of the lower mantle to $0.9\%$, of the outer core to $1.8$,
of the upper mantle to $3$, of the inner core, seen only within $11^\circ$ of straight
down, to $6$, and of the thin crust hardly at all, to $56\%$; with the
temperatures known, these are also the errors of the friction. The errors scale
as $1/\sqrt{\eta\tau}$ with the friction and the exposure time. The fourteen
picture elements counted above are what a lifetime gives for a scene that is not known in advance. A planet known to be layered
spends the same gravitons on nine numbers, as a simple scene does in the near
field (Section~\ref{sec:near-field}). Departures from spherical symmetry, from
the planet's flattening to the slabs and plumes of the mantle, would need
cameras at several places, and are tomography proper.

\paragraph{What the Earth's materials allow.} The placeholder of
Section~\ref{sec:thermal}, $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, would overstate the
deep Earth. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:glow-bound}, matching its count of gravitons needs
$0.9\,\mathrm{TPa}$ of relaxing stiffness at $2800\,\mathrm{K}$ and
$1.7\,\mathrm{TPa}$ at $5500\,\mathrm{K}$, several times the shear stiffness of
any shell. The figures of this section grant each shell all of the shear
stiffness at its base, and the core the weight of two free electrons per atom,
$0.2\,\mathrm{TPa}$, placed at the best frequency: $16$ to $34\%$ of the
placeholder's count, shell by shell. That is the most the Earth can send, not
an estimate of what it does; with the placeholder a lifetime would have brought
$1.4\times10^5$ gravitons and found the boundary of the core to within
$6\,\mathrm{km}$. Real spectra fall further below. The mantle is an insulator whose vibrations end
near $33\,\mathrm{THz}$, twice that for pairs, while at $2800\,\mathrm{K}$ its
glow would peak in number near $90\,\mathrm{THz}$; and the electrons of the
liquid iron core, with a resistivity of order a micro-ohm metre
\cite{Pozzo2012}, collide every fraction of a femtosecond and spread their
weight far beyond the band of heat, to about $3\%$ of the placeholder's
friction, a fifth of what the figures grant it. In counts the core may then
hardly stand out from the mantle. In
colour it does. The core's gravitons, from electrons whose friction is flat
across the band, carry about $2.70\,k_BT \approx 1\,\mathrm{eV}$; the mantle's,
confined below its highest vibrations, $0.09$ to $0.17\,\mathrm{eV}$. The
boundary of the core, $33^\circ$ from straight down, is a step in the colour of
the glow by a factor of five to twelve, for any resistivity between half a
micro-ohm metre and three.

\paragraph{Arrival and absorption.} The counts of this section are of gravitons
that arrive, and here the premise of an ideal pixel asks the most. A detector
made of matter absorbs through the same friction by which matter glows, and the
sum rule that bounds the glow bounds the absorption (Section~\ref{sec:thermal}). Across
the band of room-temperature heat, $k_BT/\hbar = 4\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$
wide, a metre of a solid with $30\,\mathrm{GPa}$ of relaxing stiffness stops no
more than about $1.5\times10^{-37}$ of what crosses it, and a metre of diamond
not twenty times more. A cubic metre of such a detector, crossed by one graviton
a second, would wait $2\times10^{29}$ years for its first count. Dyson, and
Rothman and Boughn, reached the same verdict for detectors of other kinds
\cite{Dyson2013,RothmanBoughn2006}. The thermal pictures of this paper are
pictures of what is there to be seen, not of what a detector made of matter
could record.

\paragraph{Counting or recording.} These budgets count gravitons, while the
array of Section~\ref{sec:no-lens} records the field at each pixel and
combines the records afterwards. In the thermal regime the two are in
tension. The radiation is so weak that each of its modes holds of order
$10^{-30}$ gravitons on average, and any device that records both quadratures
of a field at a pixel must add at least half a quantum of noise per mode
\cite{Caves1982}; recorded pixel by pixel, a thermal gravitational image would
be buried in that noise. Counting avoids the noise but loses the phase that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam} needs. For weak thermal light the same tension is known:
combining the pixels coherently before detection, as a lens does, extracts
information in proportion to the occupation, whereas recording each pixel
separately extracts it only in proportion to its square
\cite{Tsang2011,CarneyDomckeRodd2024}. A thermal gravitational camera
therefore needs either pixels joined coherently into one quantum receiver, as
quantum repeaters would join the stations of an optical interferometer
\cite{Gottesman2012}, or a
converter that turns each graviton into a photon travelling the same way, as a
strong magnetic field does, so that ordinary optics can focus it
\cite{Dyson2013}. The near field, a static field rather than radiation, is not
affected.

A counting pixel still learns where each graviton came from. One graviton of
helicity two from $\hat n$ excites the five local modes of its helicity in the
state of Eq.~\eqref{eq:square}, a coherent state of spin two
\cite{Arecchi1972}, and the measurement made of all such states, equally
weighted over the sky, is complete. It clicks at the angle $\vartheta$ from
$\hat n$ with probability density $(5/4\pi)\cos^8(\vartheta/2)$, and adds no
noise: an empty mode never clicks. Each graviton then carries $1.17$ bits about
its direction, against $0.62$ for a photon at a point sensor of light and none
for a scalar, $\log_2(2s+1) - 2s/[(2s+1)\ln 2]$ for spin $s$, and Fisher
information $s$ about each angle, half the quantum limit $2s$ for one angle
alone. A square metre of such independent pixels finds the Sun's core through
the Earth at midnight, at one graviton a second, to a degree in half an hour,
with no coherent combination at all; it sees the sky only up to degree four,
the twenty-five numbers of Section~\ref{sec:one-pixel}, which is enough for a
single bright source but not for an image.
