\section{A first image}\label{sec:first-image}

Because nothing absorbs or scatters, a thermal gravitational image is a line
integral. Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff} over frequency for constant
$\eta$ gives the energy emitted per unit volume and solid angle,
$j_E = \alpha\sigma T^4/\pi$, where $\sigma$ is the Stefan--Boltzmann constant.
Dividing by the mean energy of a thermal quantum, $2.70\,\kB T$, gives a
number emissivity $j_N$, and the brightness of the pixel that looks along the
unit vector $\hat n$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:column}
N(\hat n) = \int_0^\infty j_N\bigl(s\,\hat n\bigr)\,\dd s ,
\end{equation}
in gravitons per second, per square metre of aperture, per steradian.

Figure~\ref{fig:sun-at-midnight} evaluates Eq.~\eqref{eq:column} for an
observer on the night side of the Earth, at latitude $40^\circ$ at local
midnight. The Earth is modelled as five uniform shells with rounded textbook
radii and temperatures, from a crust at $700\,\mathrm{K}$ to an inner core at
$5500\,\mathrm{K}$. Looking straight down, the line of sight runs through
$12{,}700\,\mathrm{km}$ of rock and iron, and the expected brightness is
$6\times10^{-5}$ gravitons per second per square metre per steradian.
Lines of sight that miss the core are fainter (Fig.~\ref{fig:earth-glow}). The
Sun radiates of order $10^{8}\,\mathrm{W}$ in gravitational waves
\cite{GarciaCelyRingwald2025,Steane2024}, about
$4\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ at the Earth: roughly one graviton per
square metre per second, arriving from a patch of sky a tenth of a degree
across. Its surface brightness, about $4\times10^{5}$ gravitons per second per
square metre per steradian at the centre of the core in our model, is nearly
$10^{10}$ times that of the Earth's interior, so the Sun dominates the thermal
gravitational sky by day and by night.

The accompanying demonstration also includes the Moon. In light it is the
brightest thing in the night sky; in this image its centre is about a hundred
times fainter than the ground underfoot, because its interior is cooler,
$1200$--$1700\,\mathrm{K}$ in our model, and a line of sight through it is a
quarter as long.

The shells' radii and densities are rounded from the Preliminary Reference
Earth Model \cite{DziewonskiAnderson1981}. Two assumptions carry most of the
uncertainty. The Earth's internal friction
at thermal frequencies is not known to us, and is set to
$\eta = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ throughout as a placeholder. The Sun's
emission is spread uniformly over the inner fifth of its radius, where the
true profile is peaked toward the centre.

\paragraph{How faint.} The image is an expectation value. Figure~\ref{fig:budget}
sets the numbers of gravitons emitted side by side, and Table~\ref{tab:budget}
the numbers a camera would receive in each of the accompanying demonstrations.
The Sun delivers about one graviton per square metre per second. The whole
Earth below, in this model, delivers one every five hours, about $10^{5}$ per
square metre in a lifetime. That is a coarse picture: picking out features of
ten per cent contrast at five standard deviations takes about $2500$ counts
each, so a lifetime's exposure resolves some forty picture elements, if the
assumed friction is right. Everyday objects in a room take millions of years
for a single graviton. These counts assume no other background. If the
primordial glow of Section~\ref{sec:vistas} exists, its
$3.6\times10^{14}$ gravitons per second per square metre per steradian dwarf
every one of them; but it is cold, and an ideal filter passing only frequencies
above one terahertz leaves $10^{-20}$ of it while keeping $99.5$ per cent of the
gravitons from a body at room temperature, and more from hotter ones (with the
friction taken as constant across the band). Its fluctuations, not its known
mean, are what such a filter must remove.

\paragraph{Reading the layers back.} A camera on the surface of a round planet
needs no other viewpoint to see inside it. The line of sight at angle $\theta$
from straight down passes the centre at the distance $p = R\sin\theta$ and
crosses the planet along the whole chord at that distance, so for an emissivity
that depends only on the radius, Eq.~\eqref{eq:column} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abel}
N(p) = 2\int_p^R \frac{j_N(r)\,r\,\dd r}{\sqrt{r^2 - p^2}} ,
\end{equation}
the Abel transform of the profile $j_N(r)$, which can be inverted:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abel-inverse}
j_N(r) = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_r^R \frac{\dd N}{\dd p}\,\frac{\dd p}{\sqrt{p^2 - r^2}} .
\end{equation}
The planet's symmetry supplies the other viewpoints that tomography would need.
For shells of uniform emissivity the inversion is a peeling. The line of sight
that grazes the top of a shell crosses only that shell and those above it, so
the crust's emissivity is read first and each deeper one in turn, as in the
onion-peeling reconstructions of flames and plasmas \cite{Dasch1992}. Where the
emissivity jumps, at a boundary of radius $r$, $\dd N/\dd p$ diverges as
$(r - p)^{-1/2}$: the brightness has a kink at $\sin\theta = r/R$, and these
kinks are the steps of Fig.~\ref{fig:earth-glow}. Their places are geometry,
the radii of the boundaries; the friction, the temperatures and the exposure set
only their heights.

The heights measure a product. From the definitions above,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jN-friction}
j_N = \frac{16\,G\,\eta\,\sigma T^3}{2.70\,\kB\,c^3} ,
\end{equation}
so counted gravitons give $\eta T^3$ in each shell, and their energies give
$\eta T^4$. If the friction is flat across the thermal band, the two together
give each shell's temperature, from the mean energy $2.70\,\kB T$ of the
gravitons it sends, and then its friction. The camera is then a thermometer for
the deep Earth and a viscometer for its friction at terahertz frequencies, which
no seismometer reaches. If the friction varies across the band, each shell's
spectrum is $\alpha(\omega)$ times a Planck spectrum, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff},
and the temperature trades off against the shape of $\alpha$.

How well a lifetime does is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:earth-inverted}. It
simulates eighty years of counts on a square metre that collects from every
direction, about $1.4\times10^5$ gravitons in our model, with the Sun's path
across the ground masked, and fits five shells to them by maximum likelihood,
radii and emissivities together. In rings of one degree the fit places the
boundary of the core to within $6\,\mathrm{km}$ and the top of the lower mantle
to within $4\,\mathrm{km}$ (one standard deviation). It measures the
emissivities of the outer core and the lower mantle to $0.6$ per cent, the upper
mantle to $1.5$ per cent, the inner core, seen only within $11^\circ$ of straight
down, to $3$ per cent, and the thin crust only to $25$ per cent; with the
temperatures known, these are also the errors of the friction. The errors scale
as $1/\sqrt{\eta\tau}$ with the friction and the exposure time. The coarse
picture of the previous paragraph, forty picture elements, is what a lifetime
buys for a scene that is not known in advance. A planet known to be layered
spends the same gravitons on nine numbers, as a simple scene does in the near
field (Section~\ref{sec:near-field}). Departures from spherical symmetry, from
the planet's flattening to the slabs and plumes of the mantle, would need
cameras at several places, and are tomography proper.

\paragraph{Counting or recording.} These budgets count gravitons, while the
array of Section~\ref{sec:no-lens} records the field at each pixel and
combines the records afterwards. In the thermal regime the two are in
tension. The radiation is so weak that each of its modes holds of order
$10^{-30}$ gravitons on average, and any device that records both quadratures
of a field at a pixel must add at least half a quantum of noise per mode
\cite{Caves1982}; recorded pixel by pixel, a thermal gravitational image would
be buried in that noise. Counting avoids the noise but loses the phase that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam} needs. For weak thermal light the same tension is known:
combining the pixels coherently before detection, as a lens does, extracts
information in proportion to the occupation, whereas recording each pixel
separately extracts it only in proportion to its square
\cite{Tsang2011,CarneyDomckeRodd2024}. A thermal gravitational camera
therefore needs either pixels joined coherently into one quantum receiver, as
quantum repeaters would join the stations of an optical interferometer
\cite{Gottesman2012}, or a
converter that turns each graviton into a photon travelling the same way, as a
strong magnetic field does, so that ordinary optics can focus it
\cite{Dyson2013}. The near field, a static field rather than radiation, is not
affected.

All images shown with this paper are computed twice: by the engine that draws
them, and by an independent reference implementation that integrates
Eq.~\eqref{eq:column} by a different method. The two agree to a few parts in a
million along ordinary lines of sight.
