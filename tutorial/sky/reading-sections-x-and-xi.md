::: plain
Section X lists what a gravitational camera can do that light cannot. It sees through walls, weighs what it looks
at, and sees things that give off no light, even a beam of light. It cannot see a picture on a screen, because the
light weighs far too little. Each way of seeing has blind spots, and another way usually covers them.

Section XI looks at the sky. Near two merging black holes the camera feels two masses turning, and farther out a wave.
A star whose core collapses evenly sends no waves, but the camera weighs it lighter, by a sixth of the Sun's mass, as
the shell of neutrinos passes. In gravitational waves the Galaxy's centre, hidden from light by dust, is the brightest
part of the sky.
:::

## Most of Section X's list rests on two facts that earlier chapters derived

Section X's plain words name two facts that "lie behind most of the list". **Gravity passes through everything**,
because matter barely absorbs or scatters it (chapter
[7.8](/tutorial/geometry/wave-energy/#matter-barely-touches-the-waves)). **All energy gravitates**, because a body's
mass is its energy over $c^2$ (chapter 6.4).

The paper's Table [](/paper/#tab:affordances) lists ten **affordances**, things the camera lets its holder do, each
with its reason and regime. Every reason is derived in an earlier chapter (the ledger names them), and all but two
follow from the two facts: reading spin from the polarization (chapter
[5.4](/tutorial/waves/polarization/#two-oscillations-a-quarter-cycle-apart-make-a-pattern-that-turns)) and reading
interiors by their tremor (chapter
[4.6](/tutorial/response/fluctuation-dissipation/#each-degree-of-the-tremor-holds-one-moment-of-the-density)) follow
from neither (*read now*).

The light of a screen shows why the camera ignores pictures. A 100 W screen fills the metre in front of it with
$100\unit{W}\times(1\unit{m})/c = 3.3\times10^{-7}$ J of light, which weighs $3.7\times10^{-24}$ kg (*checked here*,
by 6.4), against the few kilograms of the screen itself.

## A beam of light pulls with twice its energy, far too faintly to show beside a person

A beam of power $P$ carries energy and an equal pressure along it. Chapter
[6.5](/tutorial/relativity/stress-energy/#what-gravitates-is-the-energy-plus-the-sum-of-the-stresses) derives that both
gravitate, so it pulls like a line of $2P/c^3$ kilograms per metre
[@TolmanEhrenfestPodolsky1931].[^bench] For 100 kW this is $7.4\times10^{-21}\unit{kg\,m^{-1}}$. Beside the beam, at the
distance $b$, the tide has the eigenvalues $(-1, 1, 0)\times4GP/c^3b^2$: a stretch along the line to the beam (a
negative eigenvalue is a stretch), a squeeze across both it and the beam, and nothing along the beam (*read now, derived
in chapter 6.5*). At a metre this is $9.9\times10^{-31}\unit{s^{-2}}$, the paper's "$10^{-30}$". A person of 70 kg a
metre away makes $9.3\times10^{-9}\unit{s^{-2}}$ (chapter
[1.4](/tutorial/newton/tides/#beside-one-mass-the-ring-stretches-along-the-line-and-squeezes-across-it)), $9.4\times
10^{21}$ times more: "a part in $10^{22}$ of the room's" (*checked here*). So the beam shows only as the difference of
readings with the beam on and off.

The magnetic part of the beam's tide is as large, and it reverses with the beam, because the flow of energy makes it
(chapter
[7.5](/tutorial/geometry/gravitomagnetism/#moving-matter-makes-the-magnetic-part-through-the-potential-of-its-flow);
*read now*). So the camera tells from the side which way an invisible beam runs. In a standing wave between two
mirrors the opposite flows cancel, and so does the magnetic part (reading chapter
[R9](/tutorial/one-point/reading-section-viii/#the-magnetic-part-equals-the-tide-in-a-wave-and-is-small-near-slow-matter);
*read now*).

## Each channel has blind spots, and another channel covers them

The paper's Table [](/paper/#tab:null) collects the **blind spots**: pairs of different scenes that one **channel**, one
way of seeing, cannot tell apart at any sensitivity. It also gives what breaks each tie (*read now*, except where
marked).

- *In the near field's electric part*, a hollow shell and a solid ball of the same mass look alike
  ([1.3](/tutorial/newton/gauss-and-poisson/#outside-a-round-body-only-its-mass-counts)), and so do the nugget, the
  flush and the skin
  ([2.5](/tutorial/outside/null-space/#the-nugget-the-flush-and-the-skin-differ-only-in-what-the-outside-cannot-read)).
  Spin breaks the tie through the moment of inertia
  ([1.6](/tutorial/newton/motion/#the-moment-of-inertia-tells-a-cored-planet-from-a-uniform-one)), and so do the
  tremor (4.6), the glow (5.6), a passing mass (13.1) and a pixel inside, where
  $\mathrm{tr}\,\curv\tE = 4\pi G\matter\rho$
  ([7.3](/tutorial/geometry/einstein-equation/#the-trace-of-the-tide-is-the-ricci-curvature-along-the-observers-time)).
- *In the near field's magnetic part*, a ball and a shell turning oppositely with no total spin look like rest, and
  rolls are silent
  ([2.8](/tutorial/outside/flows/#rolls-are-silent-outside-because-their-potential-there-is-a-gradient)). A pixel
  inside (7.5) and two tides applied in either order (13.4) break the tie.
- *In the waves*, a body moving steadily in a straight line looks like a body at rest
  ([7.6](/tutorial/geometry/gravitational-waves/#a-body-in-steady-motion-sends-no-wave-and-neither-does-an-even-pressure)),
  and a round pulsation, pressure and a smooth wheel send nothing
  ([7.7](/tutorial/geometry/quadrupole-formula/#a-swelling-sphere-and-a-smooth-wheel-send-nothing)). The near field
  of the same motion breaks the tie, except for the pulsation, which changes nothing outside (11.1).
- *In the thermal glow*, a hot body with little friction looks like a cooler one with more. A pixel counts gravitons
  in proportion to the friction $\matter\eta$ times $T^3$, with $T$ the temperature (chapter
  [5.5](/tutorial/waves/radiance/#a-materials-brightness-is-its-friction-times-a-power-of-its-temperature)), so a body
  at 600 K with an eighth of the friction sends as many as one at 300 K (*checked here*). Its energy, as
  $\matter\eta T^4$, is twice as large, and its spectrum is shifted: these break the tie.
- *In every channel* a straight cosmic string looks like empty space, because near it space has no tide; what lies
  behind it shows it (12.2).
- *One pixel* sees only the sky's degrees four and below, so twelve sources on an icosahedron look like a uniform sky
  ([9.6](/tutorial/one-point/the-sky-at-one-point/#twelve-sources-look-to-the-pixel-like-a-uniform-sky)). An array
  breaks the tie.

The passing mass is *read now, derived in chapter [13.1](/tutorial/what-a-field-keeps/push-and-pass/)*, and the two
tides are *stated in the paper* until chapter 13.4. So "the channels cover for one another": the glow places what the
near field cannot (chapter [5.6](/tutorial/waves/abel-transform/#uniform-shells-are-peeled-from-the-outside-in)), and
the near field weighs what the glow cannot.

## Near the pair the tide turns with it, and beyond three-tenths of a wavelength it is a wave

[*A pair of black holes, from the near zone to the wave zone*](/paper/#par:a-pair-of-black-holes-from-the-near-zone-to-the-wave-zone)
gives Thorne's field of a source in every zone, the paper's

{{paper eq:multipole}}

with $u = t - r/c$ the retarded time, $\hat n$ the direction from the source,[^nhat] $\matter M$ the mass, $\vect L$ the
angular momentum and $\matter I_{ij}$ the second moment of the mass, in $\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ [@Thorne1980; @Teukolsky1982]
(*stated in the paper*). Chapter 7.7 states it from Thorne, checks that it solves the wave equation and the gauge
condition, and derives its tide (*read now, derived in chapter 7.7*). For a circular orbit the trace of $\matter I_{ij}$
is constant and "changes only the gauge" (*checked here*): its part $\tfrac13\matter I_{kk}\delta_{ij}$ adds
$\tfrac16\matter I_{kk}\nabla^2(1/r) = 0$ to $\field{\bar h}^{00}$ outside the source, and nothing elsewhere, since its
time derivatives vanish.

Two black holes of $30\,M_\odot$, $d = 2200$ km apart, turn by Kepler's law at the angular speed
$\Omega = 27.3\unit{s^{-1}}$, each at $0.100\,c$. Their waves have twice the orbit's frequency, so they are
$\lambda = \pi c/\Omega = 34{,}400$ km long, and the pair has 8.8 s left before it merges (*checked here*, by 12.1's
[inspiral time](/tutorial/sky/stars/#two-compact-bodies-spiral-together-and-the-papers-pair-has-nine-seconds-left)): the
paper's "ten seconds", "a tenth of the speed of light" and "35,000 km".[^35000] The **reduced wavelength**
$\lambda/2\pi$, the wavelength over $2\pi$, is 5480 km, 2.5 times the separation. Inside it the changing tide turns
rigidly with the pair and falls as $r^{-5}$; beyond it the pattern winds into a spiral falling as $r^{-1}$ (*read now,
derived in chapter 7.7*).[^tide] In the plane of the orbit the two laws cross at $(15/16)^{1/4}c/\Omega = 0.313\lambda$,
10,800 km, the paper's "$0.3\,\lambda$" (*checked here*, by 7.7).[^plane]

An array this near resolves the two holes only if it meets chapter
[3.7](/tutorial/noise/blur-law/#what-the-law-means)'s blur law, so being near "is not enough by itself". In the near
zone the field is Newton's with an error of order $(d/r)^2$;[^error] far away it is the quadrupole wave, with an
error of order $\lambda/r$ and $|\curv\tB| = |\curv\tE|$ (*read now, derived in chapters 7.7 and
[7.6](/tutorial/geometry/gravitational-waves/#the-magnetic-part-of-a-wave-is-its-tide-turned-by-45)*).

## On the orbit's axis one pixel sees the type pass from D through II toward N

One pixel on the axis of the orbit, a distance $r$ away, reads the pair's static tide plus the wave, which the
paper writes as

$$\curv{\mathcal W} = \frac{G\matter M}{r^3}\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -2) + \sym{axis-wave-tide}{\curv h}\,\vect m_0\vect
m_0^{\mathsf T} ,\qquad \vect m_0 = (1, i, 0) ,$$

with $\curv h$ the wave's tide on the axis, in $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$ (here not a strain), a complex number whose phase
turns as $e^{-2i\Omega t}$. The static part is the whole mass's stretch $2G\matter M/r^3$ along the axis and squeeze
$G\matter M/r^3$ across it. With chapter
[9.4](/tutorial/one-point/petrov/#four-directions-solve-one-quartic)'s $\vect m(z) = (1 - z^2, i(1 + z^2), -2z)$ and
its stereographic coordinate $z$, we check the factorization of the paper's [](/paper/#eq:stars) in three lines
(*checked here*):

$$\begin{aligned}\vect m(z)^{\mathsf T}\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -2)\,\vect m(z) &= (1 - z^2)^2 - (1 + z^2)^2 - 8z^2 =
-12z^2 ,\\ \bigl(\vect m(z)\cdot\vect m_0\bigr)^2 &= \bigl(1 - z^2 - (1 + z^2)\bigr)^2 = 4z^4 ,\\
\vect m(z)^{\mathsf T}\curv{\mathcal W}\,\vect m(z) &= 4z^2\Bigl(\curv hz^2 - \frac{3G\matter M}{r^3}\Bigr) .\end{aligned}$$

This is 9.4's type II example, $\curv w(1 - 3\hat z\hat z^{\mathsf T}) + \curv c_2\,\vect m\vect m^{\mathsf T}$, with
$\curv w = G\matter M/r^3$ and $\curv c_2 = 2\curv h$, because 9.4 divides $\vect m_0$ by $\sqrt2$. Two directions
sit at $z = 0$, the wave's direction of travel; the other two sit at $z = \pm(3G\matter M/r^3\curv h)^{1/2}$. With no
wave they are at the opposite pole, and the field is of type D. As the wave outgrows the static tide they move in
toward the first two: the field is of type II at every finite distance and approaches type N only as $r \to \infty$
(*read now, derived in chapter [9.4](/tutorial/one-point/petrov/#the-pattern-of-the-four-directions-is-the-fields-petrov-type)*).[^typeN] Since $z
\propto \curv h^{-1/2}$ turns as $e^{i\Omega t}$, at the orbit's rate, the two directions turn about the line of sight
with the pair.

They cross the equator, $|z| = 1$, where $|\curv h| = 3G\matter M/r^3$, one and a half times the static stretch
$2G\matter M/r^3$ (*checked here*). That is not at $\lambda/2\pi$. On the axis the wave's tide is $|\curv h| =
8G\matter{\Delta I}\,\Omega^4/c^4r$, twice 7.7's edge-on law, with $\matter{\Delta I}$ the reduced mass ($15\,M_\odot$
here) times $d^2$. Equal to $3G\matter M/r^3$, it gives $r = 66{,}900$ km, 12 reduced wavelengths, where the paper's
estimate $(\lambda/2\pi)(c/\obs v)$ gives ten (*checked here*).

The magnetic part has no tide of the mass, only the spin's. On the axis chapter
[7.5](/tutorial/geometry/gravitomagnetism/#a-spinning-round-body-is-a-spin-dipole)'s spin dipole has the eigenvalues
$(6, -3, -3)\times G|\vect L|/cr^4$, with $|\vect L| = \matter{\Delta I}\,\Omega$ the pair's angular momentum, and a
wave's magnetic part is as large as its tide (7.6). The largest eigenvalues meet where $6G\matter{\Delta I}\,\Omega/cr^4
= 8G\matter{\Delta I}\,\Omega^4/c^4r$, that is at

$$r = \Bigl(\frac34\Bigr)^{1/3}\frac{c}{\Omega} = 1.82\,\frac{\lambda}{2\pi} ,$$

9960 km (*checked here*). Both sides hold $\matter{\Delta I}$, so this holds at any speed.[^magnetic] The table
collects where each part of the pair's field passes from its near law to the wave.

## A burst leaves a memory, largest where the oscillating wave is weakest

[*Memory*](/paper/#par:memory): radiation that escapes carries away mass, and a camera it passes is left deformed. For
energy sent out with the pattern $\dd\matter E/\dd\Omega'$, the energy per unit solid angle $\Omega'$ (a solid angle
here, not the orbit's rate $\Omega$), seen from the direction $\hat N$, the strain changes by the paper's

{{paper eq:memory}}

which chapter
[7.8](/tutorial/geometry/wave-energy/#a-burst-that-carries-energy-away-leaves-the-strain-changed-for-good) sketches at
depth B (*stated in the paper* [@Christodoulou1991; @Thorne1992]). Applied to the waves of a merger, whose power goes as
$(1 + \cos^2\iota)^2/4 + \cos^2\iota$ at the inclination $\iota$, it gives the paper's

{{paper eq:merger-memory}}

(*read now, derived in chapter [7.8](/tutorial/geometry/wave-energy/#the-waves-own-energy-leaves-a-memory-too)*, the
shape of Wiseman and Will [@WisemanWill1991]).[^axes] Edge-on it is $\tfrac{17}{24}G\matter{\Delta E}/c^4r$, where the
oscillating wave is eight times weaker in power than face-on (chapter 5.4). A camera that records the tide sees "a burst
of $\curv\tE$ whose second time integral does not return to zero" (*read now, derived in chapter
[7.8](/tutorial/geometry/wave-energy/#a-camera-that-reads-the-tide-sees-the-memory-as-a-burst-that-does-not-add-up-to-nothing)*).

The **spin memory**, an offset in the strain's time integral, goes as $\sin^2\iota\cos\iota$ (*stated in the paper*
[@PasterskiStromingerZhiboedov2016; @Nichols2017]). With $x = \cos\iota$ the pattern is $(1 - x^2)x$, whose derivative
$1 - 3x^2$ vanishes at $x = \pm1/\sqrt3$, $\iota = 54.7°$ and $125.3°$: the paper's peak, of opposite sign on the two
sides (*checked here*). The **displacement memory**, the step in the strain itself, is the same at $\iota$
and $180° - \iota$, so only the spin memory records which way the pair turned.

## A round collapse sends no waves, but the camera weighs its neutrinos as they pass

[*An exploding star*](/paper/#par:an-exploding-star). By Birkhoff's theorem the field outside a round body is that of a
point mass, whatever moves inside, so a round collapse radiates nothing (*read now, derived in chapter
[11.1](/tutorial/black-holes/schwarzschild/#outside-a-round-body-einsteins-field-is-fixed-by-the-mass-alone)*). A core
collapse frees the binding energy of the new neutron star (*read now, derived in chapter 12.1*), and the paper's "some
$3\times10^{46}$ J … a sixth of a solar mass" is $0.168\,M_\odot c^2$ (*checked here*, by 6.4). Outside the thin
shell of neutrinos the field is that of the original mass, inside it that of the remnant (*stated in the paper*
[@Vaidya1951]), so the camera "weighs the burst as it arrives", 32,600 years after the collapse for a star ten
kiloparsecs away.

While the shell passes, its energy flux $F = L/4\pi r^2$, for the luminosity $L$ in watts, squeezes the camera evenly
across the line to the star, $\curv\tE_{ij} = (GL/c^3r^2)(\delta_{ij} - \hat n_i\hat n_j)$ (*read now, derived in
chapter [7.3](/tutorial/geometry/einstein-equation/#inside-matter-the-tide-has-a-part-fixed-by-the-matter-at-that-point)*),
and the antisymmetric part of $\curv\tB$ points back to the star (*read now, derived in chapter 7.3's paper problem
and [R9b](/tutorial/one-point/reading-section-viii-2/#inside-matter-the-trace-of-the-tide-reads-the-density-there)*).
A burst of duration $\Delta t$ carries off the mass $L\Delta t/c^2$ (chapter 6.4), so the ratio of the squeeze to the
change of tide across the line is $r/c\Delta t$: $1.0\times10^{11}$ for 10 s at 10 kpc, the paper's "some $10^{11}$"
(*checked here*).[^ten]

A burst that leaves unevenly leaves memory (*read now, derived in chapter 7.8*). A narrow jet of energy $\matter E$
leaves the paper's $\Delta h = (2G\matter E/c^4r)(1 + \cos\theta)$ at the angle $\theta$ from it, up to 1 in units of
$4G\matter E/c^4r$ close to its line. On the line itself it leaves none, although $1 + \cos\theta$ is largest there:
seen along the jet, the emission is round about the line of sight, so it picks out no direction across the sky for a
strain to stretch along. Two opposite jets leave $\tfrac12$ in every direction off their axis.[^jets] A sphere and a
pure dipole leave nothing; the recoil of the remnant leaves a memory of second order in the recoil (*stated in the
paper* [@Favata2009recoil]). A burst brighter along an axis, as $1 + qP_2(\cos\theta')$ with $P_2(x) = (3x^2 - 1)/2$
and $\theta'$ the angle of emission from the axis, leaves the paper's

{{paper eq:burst-memory}}

with $\theta$ the camera's angle from the axis (*read now, derived in chapter 7.8*). For the paper's $3\times10^{46}$ J
at 10 kpc and $q = 0.01$, seen from the side, it is $4.0\times10^{-21}$, the paper's "$4\times10^{-21}$" (*checked
here*).

## In waves the Galaxy's centre is a hundred times brighter than the opposite sky

[*The Milky Way*](/paper/#par:the-milky-way): tens of millions of close white-dwarf pairs make a foreground for the
planned space detectors below a few millihertz (*stated in the paper* [@Nelemans2001]). Averaged over time and random
orientations, each pair radiates alike in every direction, so their brightness along a line of sight is the column of
stellar mass in the paper's [](/paper/#eq:column) (*read now, derived in chapter
[12.1](/tutorial/sky/stars/#in-waves-the-galaxy-shows-where-its-stars-are)*), over a model of two discs and a bulge
(*stated in the paper* [@BlandHawthornGerhard2016]).

In 12.1's model the centre is 102 times as bright as the anticentre in waves, the paper's "a hundred times". In light
the model's dust dims the centre by 31.4 magnitudes, so in that direction we see only the nearest few kiloparsecs of
stars, and the centre is only 1.33 times as bright as the anticentre (both *read now, derived in chapter 12.1*). Five
magnitudes are a factor of 100, so 30 magnitudes are $10^{12}$, the plain words' "a million million" (*checked
here*); "at the low end of the thirty to fifty" inferred from the infrared is *stated in the paper* [@Fritz2011].

At 3 mHz the waves are $c/f = 0.668$ AU long, "two-thirds of an astronomical unit", and a camera 50 AU across resolves
$0.668/50$ of a radian, $0.77°$ (*checked here*, by chapter
[5.3](/tutorial/waves/diffraction/#delaying-and-adding-the-records-of-many-pixels-points-the-array)). A year's record
tells apart frequencies $3.2\times10^{-8}$ Hz apart, so "thousands of the brightest … would each sit alone in its own
frequency bin" (the count *stated in the paper*), and one pixel places each by the paper's [](/paper/#eq:one-wave)
(*read now, derived in chapter [9.1](/tutorial/one-point/spin-two/)*).

| (e) memory | $qG\matter E/2c^4r$, $q = 0.01$, $\matter E$ of (a), from the side | $4.0\times10^{-21}$ | 7.8 |

(a) The paper's energy weighs a sixth of the Sun, and the neutrinos carry it off in about ten seconds. (b) When the
shell passes, the camera weighs the star lighter by that much: its tide along the line falls by
$1.5\times10^{-42}\unit{s^{-2}}$, $6\times10^{33}$ times less than the person's tide a metre away. (c) While the shell
passes, its squeeze is $5\times10^{10}$ to $10^{11}$ times larger, because the flux falls as $r^{-2}$ and the change
of tide as $r^{-3}$. (d) An antisymmetric $3\times3$ table holds one vector $\curv{\vect w}$, with $\curv\tB_{[ij]} =
\epsilon_{ijk}\curv w_k$, and its size is that vector's length, $4\pi GF/c^3$. It equals the squeeze of (c) because
$4\pi r^2F = L$: the same flux makes both. So one reading inside the shell names the star's direction. (e) A burst 1%
brighter along an axis changes the strain by $4.0\times10^{-21}$. A strain moves each of two free balls by half of it
times their distance (chapter
[7.6](/tutorial/geometry/gravitational-waves/#a-wave-of-strain-h-moves-each-ball-by-half-the-strain)), so balls 4 km
apart end $\tfrac12\times4.0\times10^{-21}\times4\unit{km} = 8\times10^{-18}$ m farther apart for good. The step
builds over ten seconds, so most of its signal lies below one hertz (*checked here*: a strain rising evenly over 10 s
has a spectrum falling as $f^{-2}$ above $1/(\pi\times10\unit{s}) = 0.03$ Hz), under LIGO's band, which starts near
10 Hz. (f) A perfectly round collapse leaves only the weighing and the squeeze.

By Birkhoff's theorem and Vaidya's field, outside the shell the field is still that of the original mass; only

[^bench]: The paper's "pulls like a line of matter with twice the mass" needs the bench that holds the laser and the
    absorber to be much farther from the pixel than the beam. The light pushes the absorber with the force $P/c$, so
    the bench is under that tension, which pulls like $-P/c^3$ per metre. With the bench as close as the beam a pixel
    reads $P/c^3$, the energy alone (chapter 6.5).

[^nhat]: The paper's $\hat n$ here points from the source; Sections IV, VII and VIII point it toward the source.

[^35000]: The paper's "35,000 km" is 1.6% above Newton's 34,400 km for exactly 2200 km. That is within the corrections
    to Newton's law, of order $G\matter M/c^2d$, 4% for the total mass $\matter M$ (chapter 12.1).

[^tide]: The paper's "Newton's pull of two masses … falling as $r^{-5}$": the changing pull falls as $r^{-4}$, and the
    changing tide, which the Figure plots, as $r^{-5}$. It should say "tide".

[^plane]: The paper's "$0.3\,\lambda$" holds for $\curv\tE_{zz}$ in the plane of the orbit, which its panel (c) shows;
    the next section's table gives the other crossings.

[^error]: The paper's "an error of order $(d/r)^2$" leaves out a second error, of order $(kr)^2$ with $k =
    2\pi/\lambda$, which comes from the delay. For the whole changing tide in the plane of the orbit the first is
    $0.23(d/r)^2$, from Newton's next moment, and the second $0.14(kr)^2$ (chapter 7.7). They are equal where $r^4 =
    (0.23/0.14)\,d^2/k^2$, at about 4000 km for this pair.

[^typeN]: The paper's "the type goes from D through II to N": $z^2 = 3G\matter M/r^3\curv h$ falls as $r^{-2}$ and
    reaches zero only as $r \to \infty$, so the field is of type II at every finite distance. It should say "toward
    N".

[^magnetic]: The paper's "falls at $\lambda/2\pi$ whatever the speed": the independence of the speed holds, but on the
    axis the factor is about 1.8, or 1.7 if the sizes of the whole tensors are compared.

[^axes]: The paper's $\Delta h$, in the paper's [](/paper/#eq:merger-memory) and [](/paper/#eq:burst-memory) and in
    its jet formula, is the plus part of the strain, along and across the axis as it appears on the sky; the cross part is
    zero. The paper's [](/paper/#eq:merger-memory) also assumes that all the energy, the merger's own included, leaves
    with the slow inspiral's pattern, so it is an estimate.

[^ten]: The paper's "some $10^{11}$" needs a duration that it does not state. Ten seconds, as in 1987, gives
    $1.0\times10^{11}$ across the line and $5.1\times10^{10}$ along it, where the change of tide is twice as large.

[^jets]: The paper's "two opposite jets leave the same $2GE/c^4r$" holds when the jets share $E$. With $E$ in each it
    is $4GE/c^4r$.
