Masses outside a body act on it for one instant and are gone. Each part of the body, of mass $\matter m$, receives a
kick in proportion to its mass that depends on where the part stands. Then the body is weighed. A solid ball and a
hollow shell, which pull identically on everything outside them, gain energy from the same kick in the ratio three to
five, exactly in Newton's mechanics, so the weighing sees what the steady field cannot.[^p-push]

To see why, we need a rule that says how any quantity of a body changes when every part is kicked at once. It comes
from William Rowan Hamilton's form of mechanics: the bracket of two quantities is the response of the first to an
impulse generated by the second.

::: plain
The state of a body at one moment is the position and the momentum of each of its parts. Given the state and the
forces, everything that follows is fixed. Hamilton wrote the laws of motion so that the energy gives the rate of
change of every quantity through a single operation, the Poisson bracket.

The same operation works the other way round: each quantity can push the state. The total momentum slides the body
along, the angular momentum turns it, and the energy moves it forward in time. A quantity stays constant exactly when
the push it makes leaves the energy unchanged.

A sudden tide is such a push, made by an average of the body's mass. It changes every momentum and no position. The
energy it leaves has two parts: one depends on how the body was already moving, and the other only on how the mass is
spread, which tells a solid ball from a hollow shell.
:::

## A state is a position and a momentum

A ball thrown upward and a ball falling down can pass the same point at the same moment, but what happens next
differs. To predict the motion we need the velocity too, or, as Hamilton chose, the momentum
$\sym{momentum}{\vect p} = \matter m\vect v$ of section 1.6. The **state** of a body is everything needed to predict
its motion, given the forces: for one particle, its position and its momentum.

The simplest case is a cart of mass $\matter m$ on a straight track, held by a spring of stiffness $k$, in newtons per
metre. We write $q$ for the cart's distance from the place where the spring is relaxed, in metres, and $p$ for its
momentum along the track. The state $(q, p)$ is a point in a plane with $q$ and $p$ as its axes, the cart's **phase
space**. For $N$ particles moving in three dimensions the phase space has $6N$ axes, three for the position and three
for the momentum of each. A steel ball of radius 10 cm holds about $3.5 \times 10^{26}$ atoms of iron, so its phase
space has $2 \times 10^{27}$ axes, and its state at one moment is a single point there.

The energy of the cart is its kinetic energy plus the energy stored in the spring, $\tfrac12kq^2$. Since
$\tfrac12\matter mv^2 = p^2/2\matter m$, the energy written in terms of $q$ and $p$ is

$$\sym{hamiltonian}{H} = \frac{p^2}{2\matter m} + \frac12kq^2 . \label{eq:cart}$$

The energy written as a function of the positions and momenta is called the **Hamiltonian**; it is measured in
joules. The cart keeps its energy as it moves, so its point in phase space stays on the curve where $H$ has a fixed
value $E$. Dividing $H = E$ by $E$ gives $q^2/(2E/k) + p^2/(2\matter mE) = 1$, an ellipse with the half-axes
$\sqrt{2E/k}$ along $q$, the ends of the swing, and $\sqrt{2\matter mE}$ along $p$, the momentum at its centre. The
area of an ellipse is $\pi$ times the product of its half-axes, so the area inside the cart's ellipse is

$$\pi\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}\,\sqrt{2\matter mE} = 2\pi E\sqrt{\frac{\matter m}{k}} .$$

Take a cart of 2 kg on a spring of $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$, swinging 0.1 m to each side. Its energy is
$\tfrac12 \times 50 \times 0.1^2\unit{J} = 0.25\unit{J}$, its momentum at the centre of the swing is
$1\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, and its ellipse has the area $\pi \times 0.1 \times 1 = 0.314\unit{J\,s}$. A joule second,
an energy times a time, is the unit of what physicists call an **action**.[^action]

## Hamilton's equations are Newton's law, split in two

We want the equations that move the point through phase space. Hamilton's are

$$\boxed{\;\dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\;} \label{eq:hamilton}$$

where a dot means a rate of change in time. These are **Hamilton's equations**; a system with many coordinates has one
such pair for each coordinate and its momentum.[^lagrange]

They are Newton's second law written as two equations of first order. Take $N$ particles with masses
$\matter m_a$, positions $\vect x_a$ and momenta $\vect p_a$, labelled by $a = 1, \dots, N$. We assume that **every
force comes from a potential energy $V(\vect x_1, \dots, \vect x_N)$ that depends only on the places of the parts**, as
gravity's does (section 1.6): the forces do not depend on velocity. The Hamiltonian is

$$H = \sum_a \frac{\vect p_a\cdot\vect p_a}{2\matter m_a} + V .$$

Here $\partial H/\partial\vect p_a$ is the vector of the three derivatives with respect to the components of
$\vect p_a$, and likewise for $\vect x_a$. The first equation gives $\dot{\vect x}_a = \vect p_a/\matter m_a$:
momentum is mass times velocity. The second gives $\dot{\vect p}_a = -\partial V/\partial\vect x_a$, the force on part
$a$ (section 1.2): Newton's second law.

For the cart, equations [](#eq:hamilton) give $\dot q = p/\matter m$ and $\dot p = -kq$. We differentiate the first and
put in the second, which gives $\ddot q = -(k/\matter m)\,q$. The motion $q = A\cos\omega t$ (section 1.5b) solves it,
because its second derivative is $-\omega^2A\cos\omega t$, provided that

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\matter m}} .$$

For the cart of 2 kg on $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$, $\omega = 5\unit{s^{-1}}$ and one swing there and back takes
$2\pi/\omega = 1.26\unit{s}$. With this $\omega$ the area above is $2\pi E/\omega$, and the numbers agree:
$2\pi \times 0.25/5 = 0.314\unit{J\,s}$. Drawn with $q$ to the right and $p$ upward, the point goes round its ellipse
clockwise: at the right-hand end of the swing $p = 0$ and $\dot p = -kq$ is negative, so the point moves down
(Figure [](#fig:phase)).

## The flow of states keeps every volume of phase space

A cloud of many carts, alike but started in slightly different states, fills a small patch of the phase plane, and
each cart's point moves by Hamilton's equations. We want to know how the area of the patch changes. Hamilton's
equations give every point of the plane a velocity, $\vect u = (\dot q, \dot p) = (\partial H/\partial p,
-\partial H/\partial q)$, the arrows of Figure [](#fig:phase), so the cloud moves as a fluid would.

Take a small parallelogram of states, with one corner at $(q, p)$ and its two edges the small steps
$\vect a = (a_q, a_p)$ and $\vect b = (b_q, b_p)$. Its area is the determinant $a_qb_p - a_pb_q$. The two ends of the
edge $\vect a$ move with velocities that differ, to first order, by the derivatives of $\vect u$ along $\vect a$, so
the edge changes at the rate $\dot a_q = a_q\,\partial_q\dot q + a_p\,\partial_p\dot q$ and
$\dot a_p = a_q\,\partial_q\dot p + a_p\,\partial_p\dot p$, and likewise for $\vect b$. We differentiate the area with
the product rule and put these in; the terms with $\partial_p\dot q$ and with $\partial_q\dot p$ cancel in pairs, and
what is left is

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(a_qb_p - a_pb_q\bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{\partial\dot q}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial\dot p}{\partial p}\Bigr)\bigl(a_qb_p - a_pb_q\bigr).$$

The bracket is the divergence of the flow in the phase plane. By Hamilton's equations it is

$$\frac{\partial\dot q}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial\dot p}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial^2H}{\partial q\,\partial p} - \frac{\partial^2H}{\partial p\,\partial q} = 0 ,$$

because partial derivatives commute. So every small parallelogram keeps its area, and a patch of any shape, cut into
small parallelograms, keeps its area too. With many coordinates and momenta the volume of a small box in phase space
changes at its own volume times the sum of $\partial\dot q_i/\partial q_i + \partial\dot p_i/\partial p_i$ over every
pair, and each pair gives zero in the same way:

$$\boxed{\;\text{the motion of Hamilton's equations keeps every volume of phase space.}\;}$$

This is **Liouville's theorem**. Two states never meet, because each state fixes its own future, so the states inside a
moving patch stay inside it. The number of states per unit volume of phase space, the **phase-space density**, is
therefore the same at the end of each state's path as at its start.

A cloud of free carts shows it. With no spring, $H = p^2/2\matter m$, so $\dot q = p/\matter m$ and $\dot p = 0$.
Take carts of 2 kg spread over 2 cm in position and over $0.2\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ in momentum, a square of area
$0.02 \times 0.2 = 4 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J\,s}$. After one second the fastest carts have gained
$0.2/2\unit{m} = 0.1\unit{m}$ on the slowest, so the square has sheared into a long parallelogram, but its base is
still 2 cm wide at each momentum and its height is still $0.2\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, so its area is still
$4 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J\,s}$. The cloud spreads in position while its spread of momenta stays fixed, and its
density in phase space does not change. Friction breaks the rule: a drag $-\gamma\dot q$, with $\gamma$ in
kilograms per second, adds $-\gamma p/\matter m$ to $\dot p$, so the divergence is $-\gamma/\matter m$ and areas shrink
as $e^{-\gamma t/\matter m}$. Friction is not one of Hamilton's forces, which come from a potential energy.

The theorem matters wherever many quanta travel together. The rays of a gravitational wave crossing the steady field of
a star or a galaxy are the paths of its quanta, which move by Hamilton's equations with their energy as $H$, so they
keep the volume of phase space that they fill.[^liouville-gr]
[Section 12.2](/tutorial/sky/universe/#the-graviton-sky-is-a-uniform-glow-with-a-dipole-untouched-by-lenses) uses this to
show that bending a uniform glow leaves it uniform.

## The Poisson bracket gives the rate of change of anything

We want the rate of change of any quantity $F(\vect x_1, \vect p_1, \dots)$ that depends on the state, such as the
angular momentum or an average of the mass. We use the chain rule, then equations [](#eq:hamilton) for each part:

$$\begin{aligned}
\dot F &= \sum_a\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\dot{\vect x}_a + \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\dot{\vect p}_a\Bigr)\\
&= \sum_a\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vect p_a} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vect x_a}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}$$

The combination on the right can be formed from any two quantities. The **Poisson bracket** of $F$ and $G$ is

$$\begin{aligned}
\sym{poisson-bracket}{\{F, G\}} = \sum_a\Bigl(&\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect p_a}\\
&- \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect x_a}\Bigr),
\end{aligned} \label{eq:bracket}$$

and with it the rate of change of every quantity has one form:

$$\boxed{\;\dot F = \{F, H\}.\;} \label{eq:rate}$$

This holds for any $F$ that depends on time only through the state. The bracket's unit is the unit of $F$ times the unit
of $G$, divided by a joule second, because each term divides by a position and by a momentum. Books differ in the
order of the two terms of [](#eq:bracket); this is the order used throughout this book.

For one particle, $\{x, H\} = \partial H/\partial p_x = p_x/\matter m$ and $\{p_x, H\} = -\partial V/\partial x$,
which are Hamilton's equations again. The basic brackets are $\{x, p_x\} = \{y, p_y\} = \{z, p_z\} = 1$. Every
other pair of coordinates and momenta, such as $x$ and $p_y$, or any pair from two different particles, has the
bracket 0.

The bracket has three properties that follow from its definition. It is antisymmetric, $\{F, G\} = -\{G, F\}$,
so the bracket of anything with itself is zero; in particular $\dot H = \{H, H\} = 0$: the energy is kept, unless
something outside the system, such as the masses that make a tide, makes $H$ depend on time. It is linear in each
slot. And it obeys the product rule in each slot, because derivatives do:

$$\{FG, K\} = F\{G, K\} + G\{F, K\}, \qquad \{F, GK\} = G\{F, K\} + K\{F, G\} .$$

For example, the second form gives $\{x, p^2\} = p\{x, p\} + p\{x, p\} = 2p$.

## Every quantity generates a motion, and a symmetry gives a constant

The bracket can also be read the other way round, and the kick is the reason. A sudden tide, built
[below](#an-impulsive-tide-is-the-motion-generated-by-a-moment-of-the-mass), adds to the energy for one instant the
term $-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$. Here $Q_g$ depends only on where the parts of the body are, $\epsilon$ sets the
strength of the tide, and $\delta(t)$ is a spike at $t = 0$ (section 1.3). The spike makes this term far larger than
the rest of the energy, so for that instant the state moves as Hamilton's equations say with
$-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$ in the place of $H$: for one instant, $Q_g$ does the work of the energy.

Any quantity $G$ can take the place of $H$ in this way. The motion that $G$ **generates** is the one that Hamilton's
equations give with $G$ in place of $H$:

$$\frac{\dd\vect x_a}{\dd\lambda} = \frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect p_a}, \qquad \frac{\dd\vect p_a}{\dd\lambda} = -\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect x_a} .$$

Here $\lambda$ measures how far the motion has gone, and its unit is a joule second divided by the unit of $G$. Along
this motion every quantity $F$ changes, by the same chain rule that gave equation [](#eq:rate), at the rate

$$\frac{\dd F}{\dd\lambda} = \{F, G\} . \label{eq:generated}$$

We call $G$ the **generator** of the motion. The energy generates the motion in time, with $\lambda = t$.

The total momentum along $x$, $P_x = \sum_a p_{a,x}$, generates a slide. Its bracket with the $x$ coordinate of every
part is 1, and with every other coordinate and every momentum it is 0, so the body slides as a whole along $x$ by the
distance $\lambda$, in metres.

The angular momentum about the $z$ axis, $L_z = \sum_a(x_ap_{a,y} - y_ap_{a,x})$ (section 1.6), generates a turn. For
one particle the brackets are

$$\begin{aligned}
\{x, L_z\} &= -y, & \{y, L_z\} &= x,\\
\{p_x, L_z\} &= -p_y, & \{p_y, L_z\} &= p_x ,
\end{aligned}$$

and zero for $z$ and $p_z$. A point turned anticlockwise about the $z$ axis by a small angle $\lambda$ moves from
$(x, y)$ to $(x - \lambda y,\ y + \lambda x)$, which is this rate. So $L_z$ turns the body about the $z$ axis by the
angle $\lambda$, in radians, and it turns every momentum with the positions.

We now take the bracket of a quantity $G$ with the energy. By the antisymmetry of the bracket,

$$\frac{\dd G}{\dd t} = \{G, H\} = -\{H, G\} = -\frac{\dd H}{\dd\lambda},$$

where the last derivative is along the motion that $G$ generates. A quantity that stays the same along every motion
of a system is a **constant of motion**. The equation says that $G$ is a constant of motion exactly when

$$\boxed{\;\{G, H\} = 0,\;}$$

which is exactly when the motion that $G$ generates leaves the energy unchanged. A motion that leaves the energy
unchanged is a **symmetry** of the system, and the statement that each symmetry gives a constant of motion, its
generator, is **Noether's theorem**, in Hamilton's form.

A slide does not change the momenta and a turn does not change their lengths, so neither changes the kinetic energy,
and only $V$ matters. If $V$ depends only on the differences $\vect x_a - \vect x_b$, a slide leaves it unchanged, so
the total momentum is kept. If $V$ depends only on the distances between the parts, a turn about any axis leaves it
unchanged. If it also depends on the distances from a fixed centre, only a turn about an axis through that centre
does. Either way the angular momentum about that axis is kept. These are the two conservation laws of section 1.6,
reached from the other side.

### Three tables generate every turn of an arrow

A small turn by the angle vector $\vect\theta$ moves an arrow $\vect a$ to $\vect a + \vect\theta\times\vect a$, whose
components are $a_i + \epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ja_k$ (section 1.1). Here $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the Levi-Civita symbol, not the strength of a tide. The bracket with the angular momentum $\vect L = \vect x\times\vect p$
gives the same change to each position: $\{x_i, \vect\theta\cdot\vect L\} = (\vect\theta\times\vect x)_i$, of which the
brackets with $L_z$ above are the case $\vect\theta$ along $z$. The rule can be written with three fixed tables, one
for each axis:

$$\begin{gathered}\sym{generators}{(L_k)_{ij}} = -\epsilon_{kij},\\ a_i \to a_i + \theta_k\,(L_k)_{ij}\,a_j .\end{gathered}$$

The two forms agree, because $\epsilon_{kij} = \epsilon_{ijk}$ (a cyclic move) and swapping the last two indices
changes the sign: $-\epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ka_j = \epsilon_{ikj}\theta_ka_j$, which is $\epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ja_k$ with
the summed letters renamed. For a turn about $z$, $(L_3)_{12} = -1$ and $(L_3)_{21} = 1$, so the arrow changes by
$\theta(-a_y, a_x, 0)$, as the brackets with $L_z$ gave.



A turn by a finite angle $\theta$ about the unit vector $\hat u$ is $n$ small turns by $\theta/n$, so its table is
$(1 + \tfrac{\theta}{n}\hat u\cdot L)^n$, with $\hat u\cdot L = \hat u_kL_k$ and $1$ the identity table. As $n$ grows
this tends to the series

$$\exp(\theta\,\hat u\cdot L) = 1 + \theta\,\hat u\cdot L + \frac{(\theta\,\hat u\cdot L)^2}{2!} + \dots,$$

in the same way that $(1 + x/n)^n$ tends to $e^x$. For $\hat u$ along $z$ the series sums to the table with rows
$(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, 0)$, $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$, which turns arrows anticlockwise. It is
the transpose $R^{\mathsf T}$ of the table $R$ for axes turned by the same angle (section 1.1). The same exponential
with an imaginary angle, $\exp(-i\eta\,\hat u\cdot L)$, describes a moving pixel (section 9.3).[^p-boost]
:::

## A sudden kick changes the kinetic energy in two parts

A **kick** is a force that acts for so short a time that the body does not move while it acts. Its impulse
$\sym{impulse}{\vect J} = \int\vect F\,\dd t$ in newton seconds is the change of momentum it gives (section 1.6), so a
kick changes a body's velocity from $\vect v$ to $\vect v + \vect J/\matter m$. The change of kinetic energy is the
energy after minus the energy before, with $|\vect v + \vect J/\matter m|^2 = v^2 + 2\,\vect v\cdot\vect J/\matter m +
J^2/\matter m^2$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta K &= \tfrac12\matter m\Bigl|\vect v + \frac{\vect J}{\matter m}\Bigr|^2 - \tfrac12\matter mv^2\\
&= \tfrac12\matter m\Bigl(\frac{2\,\vect v\cdot\vect J}{\matter m} + \frac{J^2}{\matter m^2}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}$$

Multiplying out gives

$$\boxed{\;\Delta K = \vect v\cdot\vect J + \frac{J^2}{2\matter m}\;} \label{eq:kick-energy}$$

in two parts. The first depends on how the body was already moving, and it can have either sign: a kick against the
motion takes energy away. The second does not depend on the motion and is never negative.

Two cases remove the first part. For a body at rest only $J^2/2\matter m$ remains, the kinetic energy of the new
velocity, as it should. And if two copies of a moving body, prepared alike, are kicked, one by $\vect J$ and one by
$-\vect J$, the first part has opposite signs in the two: the average of the two gains is the second part, and half
their difference is the first. Impulses of both signs, given to two copies and followed by two weighings, therefore
separate the two parts.

## An impulsive tide is the motion generated by a moment of the mass

We now kick every part of a body at once. Masses outside the body make, for an instant at $t = 0$, the potential

$$\field\Phi(\vect x, t) = -\epsilon\,g(\vect x)\,\delta(t),$$

where $\delta(t)$ is the delta function of section 1.3, here in time, so that $\int\delta(t)\,\dd t = 1$ and
$\delta(t)$ is in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The function $g$ is any smooth function of position that fixes the shape of the
tide, and the number $\sym{impulse-strength}{\epsilon}$ sets its strength. The product $\epsilon g$ is in square
metres per second, so that $\field\Phi$ is in joules per kilogram, and $\epsilon\nabla g$ is a velocity. For $g = x$, in metres, $\epsilon$ is a velocity, and for $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$, in square metres, it is in
$\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. We call this an **impulsive tide**. For masses outside the body $g$ is harmonic inside it (section 1.6), but the
derivation does not need this.

The minus sign is that of the potential of a point mass (section 1.2): the force on a part,
$-\matter m\nabla\field\Phi = \epsilon\,\matter m\nabla g\,\delta(t)$, points up the slope of $g$ when $\epsilon$ is
positive, as gravity pulls toward the mass that makes it.

Each part has the potential energy $\matter m_a\field\Phi(\vect x_a)$, so the Hamiltonian gains the term
$-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$, where

$$\sym{mass-moment}{Q_g} = \sum_a\matter m_a\,g(\vect x_a) = \int\matter\rho\,g\,\dd V$$

is the **moment of the mass** against $g$, with $\matter\rho$ the density, in kilograms times the unit of $g$. For
$g = 1$ it is the mass, and for $g = x$ it is $\matter Mx_{\rm cm}$ (section 1.6). We write $Q_h$ for the moment
against a harmonic $h$: these are the moments that the steady field outside a body holds (section 1.6).

We assume that **the kick lasts an instant**, so that the finite forces of $V$ add nothing while it acts, and that
**the forces inside do not depend on velocity**. During the instant, equation [](#eq:rate) with the new term gives
every quantity the rate

$$\frac{\dd F}{\dd t} = \{F, -\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g\} = -\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,\{F, Q_g\} .$$

We measure the progress of the kick by $\lambda(t) = -\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^t\delta(t')\,\dd t'$, which falls from 0
to $-\epsilon$ across the instant. Its rate is $\dd\lambda/\dd t = -\epsilon\,\delta(t)$, so dividing by it gives
$\dd F/\dd\lambda = \{F, Q_g\}$. This is equation [](#eq:generated) with $G = Q_g$. The kick is the motion that $Q_g$
generates, run from $\lambda = 0$ to $\lambda = -\epsilon$: backward by $\epsilon$, because of the minus sign of the
potential.

This motion is easy to follow. The moment $Q_g$ contains no momenta, so
$\dd\vect x_a/\dd\lambda = 0$: no part moves. And $\dd\vect p_a/\dd\lambda = -\partial Q_g/\partial\vect x_a =
-\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, which stays the same throughout, because the positions do. Running $\lambda$ to
$-\epsilon$ gives

$$\boxed{\;\vect p_a \to \vect p_a + \epsilon\,\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a),\;} \label{eq:tide-kick}$$

with every $\vect x_a$ unchanged. Each part receives the impulse $\epsilon\matter m_a\nabla g$ where it stands, a
vertical jump in phase space (Figure [](#fig:kick)).

![Three parts of a body, of equal mass, in the plane $(x, p_x)$, before the impulsive tide $g = \tfrac12x^2$ (open
circles) and after it (dots), in arbitrary units. Each state jumps straight up or down by $\epsilon\matter m\,g'(x) =
\epsilon\matter mx$, with $\epsilon\matter m = 1$; every kick points away from $x = 0$, so the jumps are orange, the
colour of a stretch.](tut-ham-kick){#fig:kick}

Any other quantity $F$ changes along the same motion. Because $\dd F/\dd\lambda = \{F, Q_g\}$ holds for every
quantity, it holds for $\{F, Q_g\}$ too, so each further derivative adds one more bracket. Taylor's theorem in
$\lambda$, taken from 0 to $-\epsilon$, gives $-\epsilon\{F, Q_g\} + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\{\{F, Q_g\}, Q_g\} - \dots$,
and antisymmetry turns each $-\{F, Q_g\}$ into $\{Q_g, F\}$:

$$\boxed{\;\Delta F = \epsilon\{Q_g, F\} + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\{Q_g, \{Q_g, F\}\} + \dots\;} \label{eq:series}$$

This is the single rule that the question asks for. Its brackets are easy to take, because $Q_g$ has no
momenta: the bracket $\{Q_g, F\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$ is the
derivative of $F$ along the kick. A check is $F = \vect p_a$: $\epsilon\{Q_g, \vect p_a\} = \epsilon\matter m_a\nabla
g(\vect x_a)$, and the series stops there, which is equation [](#eq:tide-kick).

### The energy of an impulsive tide

We want the energy $E$ that the body has after the kick, when the extra term has gone, so we take for $F$ the
Hamiltonian without it, $H_0 = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\vect p_a/2\matter m_a + V$. In the first bracket only the
derivatives of $H_0$ with respect to the momenta enter, and they come from the kinetic energy:

$$\{Q_g, H_0\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)\cdot\frac{\vect p_a}{\matter m_a} = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g(\vect x_a).$$

By equation [](#eq:rate) this is $\dot Q_g$, the rate at which the moment $Q_g$ changes.[^symm] The next bracket
takes the derivative of $\dot Q_g$ with respect to each $\vect p_a$, which is $\nabla g(\vect x_a)$:

$$\{Q_g, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\,|\nabla g(\vect x_a)|^2 .$$

This depends on the positions alone, so its bracket with $Q_g$ is zero, and the series stops at its second term.
For a body of density $\matter\rho$ the sum over the parts becomes an integral, and the energy gained is, exactly,

$$\boxed{\;\Delta E = \epsilon\,\dot Q_g + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\int\matter\rho\,|\nabla g|^2\,\dd V.\;} \label{eq:push}$$

With $\epsilon g$ in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, both terms are kilograms times $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$, which is joules.
Nothing was neglected beyond the shortness of the kick, which is why the result is exact in Newton's mechanics.

Two checks. Equation [](#eq:kick-energy) for each part, with $\vect J_a = \epsilon\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, gives
$\epsilon\,\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\matter m_a|\nabla g|^2$, and the sum over the parts is the same
result. And the uniform tide $g = x$ gives $\Delta E = \epsilon P_x + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\matter M$, the kick of the
whole body by the velocity $\epsilon$ along $x$.

### A ball and a shell gain in the ratio three to five

Take the tide $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$, which is harmonic, because $\nabla^2g = 1 - 1 = 0$. Its gradient is $\nabla g =
(x, -y, 0)$: each part is kicked outward along $x$ and inward along $y$, so the kick stretches the body along one axis
and squeezes it along the other, as a tide does. Then $|\nabla g|^2 = x^2 + y^2$, the square of the distance from the
$z$ axis, so the second term of equation [](#eq:push) is $\tfrac12\epsilon^2I_z$, where $I_z =
\int\matter\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\dd V$ is the moment of inertia about the $z$ axis (section 1.6). A body at rest gains
energy in proportion to its moment of inertia.

For a round body section 1.6 found $I = \tfrac23\int\matter\rho\,r^2\,\dd V$: $\tfrac25\matter MR^2$ for a uniform ball
and $\tfrac23\matter MR^2$ for a thin shell. The same impulsive tide therefore gives the ball and the shell energies
in the ratio $\tfrac25 : \tfrac23$, which is $3 : 5$. The two have the same field outside at any one time, and the
weighing after a kick tells them apart.

{{paper fig:hair}}

Panel (a) of the figure draws these kicks on a ball, a shell and a ball with a heavy core, with bars for the energies
gained; panel (b) belongs to Part 13.

Section 1.8 works out a realistic kick in full; here we size it. A tonne passing at $44\unit{km\,s^{-1}}$ at
$b = 1.5\unit{m}$ from the centre of a steel ball of radius $R = 0.5\unit{m}$ and mass 4110 kg gives the nearest part
of the ball a velocity of about $3 \times 10^{-12}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ in fifty microseconds. Most of it is the recoil
of the whole ball, and the tidal part, the difference across the ball, is $\epsilon R \approx 7 \times
10^{-13}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ at the edge, with $\epsilon = 1.3 \times 10^{-12}\unit{s^{-1}}$.[^tonne] We estimate the
energy as if the tide were $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$. The ball at rest gains

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta E &= \tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac25\matter MR^2 = \tfrac15\matter M(\epsilon R)^2\\
&= 3.7 \times 10^{-22}\unit{J},
\end{aligned}$$

less than a tenth of the mean kinetic energy of one atom at room temperature, $6 \times 10^{-21}\unit{J}$.

Energy has weight: a body that gains $\Delta E$ gains the mass $\Delta E/c^2$, with $c$ the speed of light (section
[6.4](/tutorial/relativity/energy-and-momentum/#a-bodys-mass-is-its-total-energy-so-heat-has-weight)). A part on the
ball's equator gains $\tfrac12(\epsilon R/c)^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-42}$ of its own weight, parts on the axis gain
nothing, and the ball gains two fifths of the equator's share, $1.0 \times 10^{-42}$, its average of
$(x^2 + y^2)/R^2$.

Does it gain the same energy both times? The readout splits the energy gained into $\epsilon\,p\,g'(q)$ and

## The bracket of two moments weighs the inside

The rule [](#eq:series) applies to any quantity, so we can ask what an impulsive tide of profile $g$ does to the rate
of change of another moment, $\dot Q_h = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla h(\vect x_a)$, which a camera that reads $Q_h$ at
two times can measure. To first order the change is $\epsilon\{Q_g, \dot Q_h\}$. The moment $Q_g$ has no momenta, so
only the first term of the bracket [](#eq:bracket) survives, and the derivative of $\dot Q_h$ with respect to
$\vect p_a$ is $\nabla h(\vect x_a)$. The change is therefore $\epsilon\sum_a\matter m_a\,\nabla g\cdot\nabla h$, with
both gradients taken at $\vect x_a$. This sum is unchanged when $h$ and $g$ swap places, so it equals
$\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\}$ too:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\} &= \sum_a\matter m_a\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,(\vect x_a)\\ &= \int\matter\rho\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,\dd V .\end{aligned}} \label{eq:two-moments}$$

So the bracket is the response of $Q_h$ to an impulse generated by $\dot Q_g$. Read with equation [](#eq:generated),
$\dot Q_g$ generates a motion that moves each part by $\lambda\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, and $Q_h$ responds at the rate
$\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\}$. The tide is the mirror of this: $Q_g$ generates it, run backward, and $\dot Q_h$ responds by
$\epsilon$ times the same integral.[^bracket-unit] With $h = g$ the bracket is the second term of equation
[](#eq:push): the energy gained is $\epsilon\dot Q_g + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\{Q_g, \dot Q_g\}$.

For $h = g = x$ the bracket is $\{\matter Mx_{\rm cm}, P_x\} = \matter M$, the total mass. For
$h = g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$ it is $\int\matter\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\dd V$, the moment of inertia, which the steady field
outside cannot see (section 1.6). For a steel ball of radius 10 cm and 32.9 kg it is
$\tfrac25 \times 32.9 \times 0.1^2 = 0.13\unit{kg\,m^2}$. Even for $h$ and $g$ harmonic, the product
$\nabla h\cdot\nabla g$ is in general not harmonic (here it is $x^2 + y^2$, whose Laplacian is 4), so the field at one
time does not hold this integral. For well-chosen pairs of harmonic $h$ and $g$ the products $\nabla h\cdot\nabla g$
include every wave $e^{i\vect k\cdot\vect x}$, so these brackets give every Fourier component of the density
(section [13.2](/tutorial/what-a-field-keeps/calderon/#two-harmonic-exponentials-multiply-into-any-ripple)). Readings
at two times hold what a reading at one time cannot.

The same integral appears twice more. In quantum mechanics the commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$ of these
two quantities is $i\hbar$ times their Poisson bracket, with $\hbar = 1.05 \times 10^{-34}\unit{J\,s}$ the reduced
Planck constant (section 8.2 builds commutators from brackets):

$$[\,Q_h,\,\dot Q_g\,] = i\hbar\int\matter\rho\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,\dd V .$$

And in a warm body the thermal motion supplies the kicks: the average of $\dot Q_h\dot Q_g$ is $k_BT$ times the same
integral, with $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant and $T$ the temperature (sections 4.2 and 4.6):

$$\langle\dot Q_h\,\dot Q_g\rangle = k_BT\int\matter\rho\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,\dd V .$$

**The idea to keep: in phase space every quantity changes at the rate of its bracket with the energy, and every
quantity generates a motion of its own. A sudden tide is the motion generated by a moment of the mass, and the energy
it leaves, exactly $\epsilon\dot Q_g + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\int\matter\rho\,|\nabla g|^2\,\dd V$, weighs what the steady
pull cannot.**

### Practice

**[1.7]** Compute $\{x, p^2\}$ and $\{p, x^2\}$ for one particle moving along a line.

::: solution
By the product rule in the second slot, $\{x, p^2\} = 2p\{x, p\} = 2p$, and $\{p, x^2\} = 2x\{p, x\} = -2x$.
:::

**[1.7]** Which quantity generates turning about a $z$ axis through a fixed centre, and why is it a constant of motion
for a particle pulled toward that centre?

::: solution
The angular momentum $L_z$. A central potential depends only on the distance from the centre, which a turn about an
axis through the centre leaves unchanged, so $\{L_z, H\} = 0$, and by Noether's theorem $L_z$ is constant.
:::

**[1.7]** In equation [](#eq:push), which term vanishes for a body at rest, and why?

::: solution
The first, $\epsilon\dot Q_g = \epsilon\sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, because every momentum is zero.
:::

**[1.7]** A uniform ball of 1 kg and radius 0.1 m, at rest, is kicked by the tide $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$ with
$\epsilon = 1\unit{s^{-1}}$. How much energy does it gain, and how much would a thin shell of the same mass and radius
gain?

::: solution
The ball gains $\tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac25\matter MR^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J}$ and the shell
$\tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac23\matter MR^2 = 3.3 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J}$, in the ratio $3 : 5$.
:::

**[1.7]** Show from Hamilton's equations that the cart's motion is $q = A\cos\omega t$, $p = -\matter m\omega A\sin\omega t$, and
check that this point moves on the ellipse $H = E$ and that the ellipse's area is $2\pi E/\omega$.

::: solution
The first equation gives $p = \matter m\dot q = -\matter m\omega A\sin\omega t$, and then
$\dot p = -\matter m\omega^2A\cos\omega t = -kq$ when $\omega^2 = k/\matter m$. The energy is
$\tfrac12\matter m\omega^2A^2\sin^2\omega t + \tfrac12kA^2\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12kA^2$ at all times, so the point
stays on the ellipse with half-axes $A$ and $\matter m\omega A$, of area $\pi\matter m\omega A^2 =
2\pi(\tfrac12kA^2)/\omega$.
:::

**[1.7]** Show that the flow of the cart on its spring has zero divergence in the phase plane, and say what happens to
a small square of states after a quarter of a swing.

::: solution
Here $\dot q = p/\matter m$ and $\dot p = -kq$, so $\partial\dot q/\partial q = 0$ and
$\partial\dot p/\partial p = 0$, and the divergence is zero. Each point goes round its ellipse in the same time
$2\pi/\omega$, so after a quarter of a swing the square has turned a quarter of the way round. Its old spread in
momentum, divided by $\matter m\omega$, is now its spread in position, and its old spread in position, times
$\matter m\omega$, is its spread in momentum; for the cart of 2 kg on $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$,
$\matter m\omega = 10\unit{kg\,s^{-1}}$. A square 1 cm wide and $0.1\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ tall becomes a rectangle
$0.1/10\unit{m} = 1$ cm wide in position and $10 \times 0.01 = 0.1\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ tall in momentum: the same
area, $10^{-3}\unit{J\,s}$.
:::

### Stretch

**[1.7]** Show that the rates of change of two moments have the bracket
$\{\dot Q_h, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot[(\nabla\nabla h)\nabla g - (\nabla\nabla g)\nabla h]$, with both
Hessians and both gradients taken at $\vect x_a$, where $\nabla\nabla h$ is the Hessian of $h$ (section 1.4). Show
that the bracket vanishes for $h = x$, $g = y$.

::: solution
The derivative of $\dot Q_h = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla h(\vect x_a)$ with respect to $\vect x_a$ is
$(\nabla\nabla h)\,\vect p_a$, and with respect to $\vect p_a$ it is $\nabla h(\vect x_a)$. So $\{\dot Q_h, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a[(\nabla\nabla h)\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g - \nabla h\cdot(\nabla\nabla
g)\vect p_a]$. A Hessian is symmetric, which gives the stated form. For $h = x$ and $g = y$ both Hessians are zero.
:::

**[1.7]** The cart of 2 kg on its spring of $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$ also feels a drag $-\gamma\dot q$ with
$\gamma = 0.1\unit{kg\,s^{-1}}$. How long does a patch of its states take to shrink to $1/e$ of its area, and how many
swings is that? What does the shrinking say about a cloud of such carts?

::: solution
The drag adds $-\gamma p/\matter m$ to $\dot p$, so the divergence is $-\gamma/\matter m = -0.05\unit{s^{-1}}$ and
the area falls as $e^{-0.05t}$, to $1/e$ in 20 s, which is $20/1.26 = 16$ swings. Every cart spirals in toward the state
of rest, so the whole cloud crowds toward one point of the phase plane, and its density in phase space grows without
limit, which Hamilton's equations never allow.
:::

### The camera

**[1.7]** The gradients of harmonic functions at the $N$ places of a body's parts can take any values (a fact of
harmonic analysis that we do not prove). Use this to show that a quantity $F$ whose bracket
with every moment $Q_h$ vanishes does not depend on the momenta, and that if its bracket with $H$ also vanishes, it is
a constant.

::: solution
By the bracket [](#eq:bracket), $\{F, Q_h\} = -\sum_a\matter m_a\nabla h(\vect x_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$.
The vectors $\matter m_a\nabla h(\vect x_a)$ can be chosen at will, so this can vanish for
every $h$ only if every $\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$ is zero: $F$ depends on the places alone. Then
$\{F, H\} = \sum_a(\vect p_a/\matter m_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect x_a$. This must vanish for every state, so every $\partial F/\partial\vect x_a$ is zero and $F$ is a constant.
:::
