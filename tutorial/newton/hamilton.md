The paper's [Section XIII](/paper/#sec:second-order) proposes an experiment on a body whose inside the camera cannot
see. Masses outside the body act on it for one instant and are gone. Each part of the body, of mass $\matter m$,
receives a kick in proportion to its mass, "where it stands". Then the body is weighed. The paper finds that a solid
ball and a hollow shell, which have the same mass and pull identically on everything outside them, gain energy from
the same kick in the ratio three to five. It says that its formula for the energy gained, equation
[](/paper/#eq:push), holds "exactly in Newton's mechanics".

To see why, we need a rule that says how any quantity of a body changes when every part is kicked at once: its
energy, its momentum, or one of the averages of its mass that the camera reads. This chapter asks for that single
rule. It comes from William Rowan Hamilton's form of mechanics, which the paper uses when it says that "the bracket of
two quantities is the response of the first to an impulse generated by the second".

::: plain
The state of a body at one moment is the position and the momentum of each of its parts. If you know both, and the
forces, you can predict everything that follows. Hamilton wrote the laws of motion so that the energy gives the rate
of change of every quantity through a single operation, the Poisson bracket.

The same operation works the other way round. Each quantity can be used to push the state: the total momentum slides
the body along, the angular momentum turns it, and the energy moves it forward in time. A quantity stays constant
exactly when the push it makes leaves the energy unchanged.

A sudden tide is such a push, made by an average of the body's mass. It changes every momentum and no position. The
energy it leaves has two parts. One depends on how the body was already moving. The other depends only on how the mass
is spread, and it tells a solid ball from a hollow shell that pull alike on everything outside them.
:::

## A state is a position and a momentum

A ball thrown upward and a ball falling down can pass the same point at the same moment. Their positions agree, but
what happens next does not. To predict the motion we need the velocity too, or, as Hamilton chose, the momentum
$\sym{momentum}{\vect p} = \matter m\vect v$ of chapter 1.6. The **state** of a body is everything needed to predict
its motion, given the forces: for one particle, its position and its momentum.

The simplest case is a cart of mass $\matter m$ on a straight track, held by a spring of stiffness $k$, in newtons per
metre. We write $q$ for the cart's distance from the place where the spring is relaxed, in metres, and $p$ for its
momentum along the track. The state $(q, p)$ is a point in a plane with $q$ and $p$ as its axes, the cart's **phase
space**. For $N$ particles moving in three dimensions the phase space has $6N$ axes, three for the position and three
for the momentum of each. The paper's steel ball of radius 10 cm holds about $3.5 \times 10^{26}$ atoms of iron, so
its phase space has $2 \times 10^{27}$ axes, and its state at one moment is a single point there.

The energy of the cart is its kinetic energy plus the energy stored in the spring, $\tfrac12kq^2$. Since
$\tfrac12\matter mv^2 = p^2/2\matter m$, the energy written in terms of $q$ and $p$ is

$$\sym{hamiltonian}{H} = \frac{p^2}{2\matter m} + \frac12kq^2 . \label{eq:cart}$$

The energy written as a function of the positions and momenta is called the **Hamiltonian**; it is measured in
joules. The cart keeps its energy as it moves, so its point in phase space stays on the curve where $H$ has a fixed
value $E$. Dividing $H = E$ by $E$ gives $q^2/(2E/k) + p^2/(2\matter mE) = 1$, an ellipse with the half-axes
$\sqrt{2E/k}$ along $q$, the ends of the swing, and $\sqrt{2\matter mE}$ along $p$, the momentum at its centre. The
area of an ellipse is $\pi$ times the product of its half-axes, so the area inside the cart's ellipse is

$$\pi\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}\,\sqrt{2\matter mE} = 2\pi E\sqrt{\frac{\matter m}{k}} .$$

Take a cart of 2 kg on a spring of $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$, swinging 0.1 m to each side. Its energy is
$\tfrac12 \times 50 \times 0.1^2\unit{J} = 0.25\unit{J}$, its momentum at the centre of the swing is
$1\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, and its ellipse has the area $\pi \times 0.1 \times 1 = 0.314\unit{J\,s}$. A joule second,
an energy times a time, is the unit of what physicists call an **action**.[^action]

## Hamilton's equations are Newton's law, split in two

We want the equations that move the point through phase space. Hamilton's are

$$\boxed{\;\dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\;} \label{eq:hamilton}$$

where a dot means a rate of change in time, as in chapter 1.6. These are **Hamilton's equations**. A system with many
coordinates has one such pair for each coordinate and its momentum.[^lagrange]

They are Newton's second law written as two equations of first order. Take $N$ particles with masses
$\matter m_a$, positions $\vect x_a$ and momenta $\vect p_a$, labelled by $a = 1, \dots, N$. We assume that **every
force comes from a potential energy $V(\vect x_1, \dots, \vect x_N)$ that depends only on the places of the parts**, as
gravity's does in chapter 1.6. This is what the paper means by "matter whose forces do not depend on velocity". The
Hamiltonian is

$$H = \sum_a \frac{\vect p_a\cdot\vect p_a}{2\matter m_a} + V .$$

Here $\partial H/\partial\vect p_a$ means the vector of the three derivatives with respect to the components of
$\vect p_a$, and likewise for $\vect x_a$. The first equation gives $\dot{\vect x}_a = \vect p_a/\matter m_a$: momentum
is mass times velocity. The second gives $\dot{\vect p}_a = -\partial V/\partial\vect x_a$, and minus that gradient is
the force on part $a$ (chapter 1.2), so the force changes the momentum: Newton's second law.

For the cart, equations [](#eq:hamilton) give $\dot q = p/\matter m$ and $\dot p = -kq$. We differentiate the first and
put in the second, which gives $\ddot q = -(k/\matter m)\,q$. The motion $q = A\cos\omega t$ of chapter 1.5b solves it,
because its second derivative is $-\omega^2A\cos\omega t$, provided that

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\matter m}} .$$

For the cart of 2 kg on $50\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$, $\omega = 5\unit{s^{-1}}$ and one swing there and back takes
$2\pi/\omega = 1.26\unit{s}$. With this $\omega$ the area of the last section is $2\pi E/\omega$, and the numbers
agree: $2\pi \times 0.25/5 = 0.314\unit{J\,s}$. Drawn with $q$ to the right and $p$ upward, the point goes round its
ellipse clockwise: at the right-hand end of the swing $p = 0$ and $\dot p = -kq$ is negative, so the point moves down.

## The Poisson bracket gives the rate of change of anything

We want the rate of change of any quantity $F(\vect x_1, \vect p_1, \dots)$ that depends on the state, such as the
angular momentum or an average of the mass. We use the chain rule, then equations [](#eq:hamilton) for each part:

$$\begin{aligned}
\dot F &= \sum_a\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\dot{\vect x}_a + \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\dot{\vect p}_a\Bigr)\\
&= \sum_a\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vect p_a} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vect x_a}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}$$

The combination on the right can be formed from any two quantities. The **Poisson bracket** of $F$ and $G$ is

$$\begin{aligned}
\sym{poisson-bracket}{\{F, G\}} = \sum_a\Bigl(&\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect x_a}\cdot\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect p_a}\\
&- \frac{\partial F}{\partial\vect p_a}\cdot\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect x_a}\Bigr),
\end{aligned} \label{eq:bracket}$$

and with it the rate of change of every quantity has one form:

$$\boxed{\;\dot F = \{F, H\}.\;} \label{eq:rate}$$

This holds for any $F$ that depends on time only through the state. The bracket's unit is the unit of $F$ times the unit
of $G$, divided by a joule second, because each term divides by a position and by a momentum. Books differ in the
order of the two terms of [](#eq:bracket); this is the paper's order, which its Appendix C uses.

For one particle, $\{x, H\} = \partial H/\partial p_x = p_x/\matter m$ and $\{p_x, H\} = -\partial V/\partial x$,
which are Hamilton's equations again. The basic brackets are $\{x, p_x\} = \{y, p_y\} = \{z, p_z\} = 1$. Every
other pair of coordinates and momenta, such as $x$ and $p_y$, or any pair from two different particles, has the
bracket 0.

The bracket has three properties that follow from its definition. It is antisymmetric, $\{F, G\} = -\{G, F\}$,
so the bracket of anything with itself is zero. In particular $\dot H = \{H, H\} = 0$: the energy is kept, unless
something outside the system, such as the masses that make a tide, makes $H$ depend on time. It is linear in each
slot. And it obeys the product rule in each slot, because derivatives do:

$$\{FG, K\} = F\{G, K\} + G\{F, K\}, \qquad \{F, GK\} = G\{F, K\} + K\{F, G\} .$$

For example, the second form gives $\{x, p^2\} = p\{x, p\} + p\{x, p\} = 2p$.

## Every quantity generates a motion, and a symmetry gives a constant

The bracket can also be read the other way round, and the paper's kick is the reason. A sudden tide, built
[below](#an-impulsive-tide-is-the-motion-generated-by-a-moment-of-the-mass), adds to the energy for one instant the
term $-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$. Here $Q_g$ depends only on where the parts of the body are, $\epsilon$ sets the
strength of the tide, and $\delta(t)$ is a spike at $t = 0$ (chapter 1.3). The spike makes this term far larger than
the rest of the energy, so for that instant the state moves as Hamilton's equations say with
$-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$ in the place of $H$: for one instant, $Q_g$ does the work of the energy.

Any quantity $G$ can take the place of $H$ in this way. The motion that $G$ **generates** is the one that Hamilton's
equations give with $G$ in place of $H$:

$$\frac{\dd\vect x_a}{\dd\lambda} = \frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect p_a}, \qquad \frac{\dd\vect p_a}{\dd\lambda} = -\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vect x_a} .$$

Here $\lambda$ measures how far the motion has gone, and its unit is a joule second divided by the unit of $G$. Along
this motion every quantity $F$ changes, by the same chain rule that gave equation [](#eq:rate), at the rate

$$\frac{\dd F}{\dd\lambda} = \{F, G\} . \label{eq:generated}$$

We call $G$ the **generator** of the motion. The energy generates the motion in time, with $\lambda = t$.

The total momentum along $x$, $P_x = \sum_a p_{a,x}$, generates a slide. Its bracket with the $x$ coordinate of every
part is 1, and with every other coordinate and every momentum it is 0, so the body slides as a whole along $x$ by the
distance $\lambda$, in metres.

The angular momentum about the $z$ axis, $L_z = \sum_a(x_ap_{a,y} - y_ap_{a,x})$ (chapter 1.6), generates a turn. For
one particle the brackets are

$$\begin{aligned}
\{x, L_z\} &= -y, & \{y, L_z\} &= x,\\
\{p_x, L_z\} &= -p_y, & \{p_y, L_z\} &= p_x ,
\end{aligned}$$

and zero for $z$ and $p_z$. A point turned anticlockwise about the $z$ axis by a small angle $\lambda$ moves from
$(x, y)$ to $(x - \lambda y,\ y + \lambda x)$, which is this rate. So $L_z$ turns the body about the $z$ axis by the
angle $\lambda$, in radians, and it turns every momentum with the positions.

We now take the bracket of a quantity $G$ with the energy. By the antisymmetry of the bracket,

$$\frac{\dd G}{\dd t} = \{G, H\} = -\{H, G\} = -\frac{\dd H}{\dd\lambda},$$

where the last derivative is along the motion that $G$ generates. A quantity that stays the same along every motion
of a system is a **constant of motion**. The equation says that $G$ is a constant of motion exactly when

$$\boxed{\;\{G, H\} = 0,\;}$$

which is exactly when the motion that $G$ generates leaves the energy unchanged. A motion that leaves the energy
unchanged is a **symmetry** of the system, and the statement that each symmetry gives a constant of motion, its
generator, is **Noether's theorem**, in Hamilton's form.

A slide does not change the momenta and a turn does not change their lengths, so neither changes the kinetic energy,
and only $V$ matters. If $V$ depends only on the differences $\vect x_a - \vect x_b$, a slide leaves it unchanged, so
the total momentum is kept. If $V$ depends only on the distances between the parts, a turn about any axis leaves it
unchanged. If it also depends on the distances from a fixed centre, only a turn about an axis through that centre
does. Either way the angular momentum about that axis is kept. These are chapter 1.6's two conservation laws, reached
from the other side.

### Three tables generate every turn of an arrow

Chapter 1.1 showed that a small turn by the angle vector $\vect\theta$ moves an arrow $\vect a$ to
$\vect a + \vect\theta\times\vect a$, whose components are $a_i + \epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ja_k$. Here $\epsilon_{ijk}$,
with three indices, is the Levi-Civita symbol of chapter 1.1, not the strength of a tide. The bracket with the angular
momentum $\vect L = \vect x\times\vect p$ gives the same change to each position: $\{x_i, \vect\theta\cdot\vect L\} =
(\vect\theta\times\vect x)_i$, of which the brackets with $L_z$ above are the case $\vect\theta$ along $z$. The paper
writes this rule with three fixed tables, one for each axis:

$$\begin{gathered}\sym{generators}{(L_k)_{ij}} = -\epsilon_{kij},\\ a_i \to a_i + \theta_k\,(L_k)_{ij}\,a_j .\end{gathered}$$

The two forms agree, because $\epsilon_{kij} = \epsilon_{ijk}$ (a cyclic move) and swapping the last two indices
changes the sign: $-\epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ka_j = \epsilon_{ikj}\theta_ka_j$, which is $\epsilon_{ijk}\theta_ja_k$ with
the summed letters renamed. For a turn about $z$, $(L_3)_{12} = -1$ and $(L_3)_{21} = 1$, so the arrow changes by
$\theta(-a_y, a_x, 0)$, as the brackets with $L_z$ gave.

The paper uses the letter $L$ for these tables and for the angular momentum. The angular momentum $L_z$ is a number
that depends on the state; $(L_k)_{ij}$, with two more indices, is a fixed table of pure numbers.

A turn by a finite angle $\theta$ about the unit vector $\hat u$ is $n$ small turns by $\theta/n$, so its table is
$(1 + \tfrac{\theta}{n}\hat u\cdot L)^n$, with $\hat u\cdot L = \hat u_kL_k$ and $1$ the identity table. As $n$ grows
this tends to the series

$$\exp(\theta\,\hat u\cdot L) = 1 + \theta\,\hat u\cdot L + \frac{(\theta\,\hat u\cdot L)^2}{2!} + \dots,$$

in the same way that $(1 + x/n)^n$ tends to $e^x$. For $\hat u$ along $z$ the series sums to the table with rows
$(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, 0)$, $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$, which turns arrows anticlockwise. It is
the transpose $R^{\mathsf T}$ of chapter 1.1's table $R$ for axes turned by the same angle: the paper's "rotation that
carries the old axes onto the new" (chapter 1.1, its margin note on the paper's turns). The paper's
[Section VIII](/paper/#sec:one-pixel) writes a turned reading as $R^{\mathsf T}\mathcal W R$ and uses the exponential
only for a moving pixel, $\Lambda = \exp(-i\eta\,\hat u\cdot L)$, a turn by an imaginary angle; chapter 9.3 takes it up.
:::

## A sudden kick changes the kinetic energy in two parts

A **kick** is a force that acts for so short a time that the body does not move while it acts. Chapter 1.6 defined the
impulse of a force, its integral over time, $\sym{impulse}{\vect J} = \int\vect F\,\dd t$, in newton seconds,
which by the second law is the change of momentum it gives. A kick therefore changes a body's velocity from $\vect v$
to $\vect v + \vect J/\matter m$. We want the change of kinetic energy, so we subtract the energy before from the
energy after and expand the square as $|\vect v + \vect J/\matter m|^2 = v^2 + 2\,\vect v\cdot\vect J/\matter m +
J^2/\matter m^2$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta K &= \tfrac12\matter m\Bigl|\vect v + \frac{\vect J}{\matter m}\Bigr|^2 - \tfrac12\matter mv^2\\
&= \tfrac12\matter m\Bigl(\frac{2\,\vect v\cdot\vect J}{\matter m} + \frac{J^2}{\matter m^2}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}$$

Multiplying out gives

$$\boxed{\;\Delta K = \vect v\cdot\vect J + \frac{J^2}{2\matter m}\;} \label{eq:kick-energy}$$

in two parts. The first depends on how the body was already moving, and it can have either sign: a kick against the
motion takes energy away. The second does not depend on the motion and is never negative.

Two cases remove the first part, and the paper uses both. For a body at rest only $J^2/2\matter m$ remains, the
kinetic energy of the new velocity, as it should. And if two copies of a moving body, prepared alike, are kicked, one
by $\vect J$ and one by $-\vect J$, the first part has opposite signs in the two and cancels in their average. The
paper's "impulses of both signs, given to two copies of the body prepared alike, and two weighings" work in this way:
the average of the two gains gives the second part, and half their difference gives the first.

## An impulsive tide is the motion generated by a moment of the mass

We now kick every part of a body at once. Masses outside the body make, for an instant at $t = 0$, the potential

$$\field\Phi(\vect x, t) = -\epsilon\,g(\vect x)\,\delta(t),$$

where $\delta(t)$ is the one-dimensional delta function of chapter 1.3, here in time, so that $\int\delta(t)\,\dd t = 1$
and $\delta(t)$ is measured in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The function $g$ is any smooth function of position that fixes the
shape of the tide, and the number $\sym{impulse-strength}{\epsilon}$ sets its strength. The product $\epsilon g$ is
in square metres per second, so that $\field\Phi$ is in joules per kilogram, and $\epsilon\nabla g$ is a velocity.
The unit of $\epsilon$ alone depends on that of $g$: for $g = x$, in metres, $\epsilon$ is a velocity, and for
$g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$, in square metres, it is in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. We call this an **impulsive tide**. In the
paper $g$ is harmonic, because the potential of masses outside a body is harmonic inside it (chapter 1.6), but the
derivation below does not need this.

The minus sign is the same as in the potential of a point mass (chapter 1.2). The force on a part, $-\matter
m\nabla\field\Phi = \epsilon\,\matter m\nabla g\,\delta(t)$, points up the slope of $g$ when $\epsilon$ is positive, as
gravity pulls toward the mass that makes it.

Each part has the potential energy $\matter m_a\field\Phi(\vect x_a)$, so the Hamiltonian gains the term
$-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g$, where

$$\sym{mass-moment}{Q_g} = \sum_a\matter m_a\,g(\vect x_a) = \int\matter\rho\,g\,\dd V$$

is the **moment of the mass** against $g$, with $\matter\rho$ the density, measured in kilograms times the unit of $g$.
For $g = 1$ it is the mass, and for $g = x$ it is $\matter Mx_{\rm cm}$ (chapter 1.6). The paper writes $Q_h$ for the
moment against a harmonic $h$, and for harmonic $h$ these moments are what the steady field outside a body holds
(chapter 1.6).

We assume that **the kick lasts an instant**, so that the finite forces of $V$ add nothing while it acts, and that
**the forces inside do not depend on velocity**. During the instant, equation [](#eq:rate) with the new term gives
every quantity the rate

$$\frac{\dd F}{\dd t} = \{F, -\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,Q_g\} = -\epsilon\,\delta(t)\,\{F, Q_g\} .$$

We measure the progress of the kick by $\lambda(t) = -\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^t\delta(t')\,\dd t'$, which falls from 0
to $-\epsilon$ across the instant. Its rate is $\dd\lambda/\dd t = -\epsilon\,\delta(t)$, so dividing by it gives
$\dd F/\dd\lambda = \{F, Q_g\}$. This is equation [](#eq:generated) with $G = Q_g$. The kick is the motion that $Q_g$
generates, run from $\lambda = 0$ to $\lambda = -\epsilon$: backward by $\epsilon$, because of the minus sign of the
potential.

This motion is easy to follow. The moment $Q_g$ contains no momenta, so
$\dd\vect x_a/\dd\lambda = 0$: no part moves. And $\dd\vect p_a/\dd\lambda = -\partial Q_g/\partial\vect x_a =
-\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, which stays the same throughout, because the positions do. Running $\lambda$ to
$-\epsilon$ gives

$$\boxed{\;\vect p_a \to \vect p_a + \epsilon\,\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a),\;} \label{eq:tide-kick}$$

with every $\vect x_a$ unchanged. This is the paper's "impulse $\epsilon m\nabla g$ where it stands", a vertical jump
in phase space.

Any other quantity $F$ changes along the same motion. Because $\dd F/\dd\lambda = \{F, Q_g\}$ holds for every
quantity, it holds for $\{F, Q_g\}$ too, so the second derivative is $\{\{F, Q_g\}, Q_g\}$, and each further
derivative adds one more bracket. Taylor's theorem in $\lambda$, taken from 0 to $-\epsilon$, gives
$-\epsilon\{F, Q_g\} + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\{\{F, Q_g\}, Q_g\} - \dots$, and antisymmetry turns each $-\{F, Q_g\}$ into
$\{Q_g, F\}$:

$$\boxed{\;\Delta F = \epsilon\{Q_g, F\} + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\{Q_g, \{Q_g, F\}\} + \dots\;} \label{eq:series}$$

This is the single rule that the chapter's question asks for. Its brackets are easy to take, because $Q_g$ has no
momenta: the bracket $\{Q_g, F\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$ is the
derivative of $F$ along the kick. A check is $F = \vect p_a$: $\epsilon\{Q_g, \vect p_a\} = \epsilon\matter m_a\nabla
g(\vect x_a)$, and the series stops there, which is equation [](#eq:tide-kick).

### The energy of an impulsive tide

We want the energy $E$ that the body has after the kick, when the extra term has gone, so we take for $F$ the
Hamiltonian without it, $H_0 = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\vect p_a/2\matter m_a + V$. In the first bracket only the
derivatives of $H_0$ with respect to the momenta enter, and they come from the kinetic energy:

$$\{Q_g, H_0\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)\cdot\frac{\vect p_a}{\matter m_a} = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g(\vect x_a).$$

By equation [](#eq:rate) this is $\dot Q_g$, the rate at which the moment $Q_g$ changes.[^symm] The next bracket
takes the derivative of $\dot Q_g$ with respect to each $\vect p_a$, which is $\nabla g(\vect x_a)$:

$$\{Q_g, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a\matter m_a\,|\nabla g(\vect x_a)|^2 .$$

This depends on the positions alone, so its bracket with $Q_g$ is zero, and the series stops at its second term.
For a body of density $\matter\rho$ the sum over the parts becomes an integral, and the energy gained is the paper's
equation, exactly:

{{paper eq:push}}

With $\epsilon g$ in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, both terms are kilograms times $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$, which is joules.
Nothing was neglected beyond the shortness of the kick, which is why the result is exact in Newton's mechanics.

We check the result against what we already know. First, equation [](#eq:kick-energy) for each part, with
$\vect J_a = \epsilon\matter m_a\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, gives $\epsilon\,\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g +
\tfrac12\epsilon^2\matter m_a|\nabla g|^2$, and the sum over the parts is the same result: the bracket series and the
elementary rule agree. Second, the uniform tide $g = x$ gives $\Delta E = \epsilon P_x + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\matter M$,
the kick of the whole body by the velocity $\epsilon$ along $x$.

### A ball and a shell gain in the ratio three to five

Take the tide $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$, which is harmonic, because $\nabla^2g = 1 - 1 = 0$. Its gradient is
$\nabla g = (x, -y, 0)$: each part is kicked outward along $x$ and inward along $y$, so the kick stretches the body
along one axis and squeezes it along the other, as a tide does. Then $|\nabla g|^2 = x^2 + y^2$, the square of the
distance from the $z$ axis, so the second term of equation [](/paper/#eq:push) is $\tfrac12\epsilon^2I_z$, where
$I_z = \int\matter\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\dd V$ is the moment of inertia about the $z$ axis of chapter 1.6. A body at rest
gains energy in proportion to its moment of inertia.

For a round body chapter 1.6 found $I = \tfrac23\int\matter\rho\,r^2\,\dd V$: $\tfrac25\matter MR^2$ for a uniform ball
and $\tfrac23\matter MR^2$ for a thin shell. The same impulsive tide therefore gives the ball and the shell energies
in the ratio $\tfrac25 : \tfrac23$, which is $3 : 5$, the paper's ratio. The two have the same field outside at any one
time, and the weighing after a kick tells them apart.

The paper sizes such a kick with the scene of its next paragraph, which chapter 1.8 works out in full. A tonne,
$\matter\mu = 1000\unit{kg}$, passes at $V = 44\unit{km\,s^{-1}}$ one metre from the edge of a steel ball of radius $R =
0.5\unit{m}$ and mass 4110 kg, at $b = 1.5\unit{m}$ from its centre. With $G$ here the gravitational constant, $6.67
\times 10^{-11}\unit{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, and not a generator, the nearest part of the ball feels the pull
$G\matter\mu/(1\unit{m})^2 = 6.7 \times 10^{-8}\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ for about the $45\unit{\mu s}$ that the tonne takes to
cross two metres, and so gains about $3 \times 10^{-12}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$. This is the paper's $\Delta v$, given "in
fifty microseconds". At that speed a part would take ten years to move a millimetre.

Most of that velocity is not a tide. The centre of the ball gains $2 \times 10^{-12}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ in the same
direction, and that much is the recoil of the whole ball. The tide is the difference across the ball. By chapter 1.4
it stretches along the line to the tonne at the rate $2G\matter\mu/b^3 = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\unit{s^{-2}}$ at the
closest approach, which gives the edge $2.0 \times 10^{-8}\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ relative to the centre, for about
$b/V = 34\unit{\mu s}$. The tidal kick at the edge is therefore about $7 \times 10^{-13}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$, less than a
quarter of the whole.[^tonne]

The tonne's tide is not exactly $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$, but we can estimate the energy as if it were, with $x$
toward the tonne's path and $\epsilon = 1.3 \times 10^{-12}\unit{s^{-1}}$, so that $\epsilon R$ is that tidal kick.
The ball at rest gains

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta E &= \tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac25\matter MR^2 = \tfrac15\matter M(\epsilon R)^2\\
&= 3.7 \times 10^{-22}\unit{J},
\end{aligned}$$

less than a tenth of the mean kinetic energy of a single atom at room temperature, $6 \times 10^{-21}\unit{J}$. Chapter
1.8 finds $4.0 \times 10^{-22}\unit{J}$ from the whole pass. The paper warns that no tonne could be brought up and taken
away in fifty microseconds, and this is why its next paragraph, and chapter 1.8, let the tonne fly past instead.

Energy has weight: a body that gains the energy $\Delta E$ gains the mass $\Delta E/c^2$, with $c$ the speed of light
(stated here; chapter 6.4 derives it). A part on the ball's equator, at the distance $R$ from the $z$ axis, gains
$\tfrac12(\epsilon R/c)^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-42}$ of its own weight, and parts on the axis gain nothing. The ball
gains two fifths of the equator's share, $1.0 \times 10^{-42}$, because two fifths is its average of
$(x^2 + y^2)/R^2$. The paper's "five parts in $10^{41}$" is $\tfrac12(\Delta v/c)^2$ for the whole
$\Delta v$ of $3 \times 10^{-12}\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$, recoil included: fifty times the share of the inside.

## The bracket of two moments weighs the inside

The rule [](#eq:series) applies to any quantity, so we can ask what an impulsive tide of profile $g$ does to the rate
of change of another moment, $\dot Q_h = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla h(\vect x_a)$, which a camera that reads $Q_h$ at
two times can measure. To first order the change is $\epsilon\{Q_g, \dot Q_h\}$. The moment $Q_g$ has no momenta, so
only the first term of the bracket [](#eq:bracket) survives, and the derivative of $\dot Q_h$ with respect to
$\vect p_a$ is $\nabla h(\vect x_a)$. The change is therefore $\epsilon\sum_a\matter m_a\,\nabla g\cdot\nabla h$, with
both gradients taken at $\vect x_a$.

This sum does not change when $h$ and $g$ swap places. The paper writes the bracket with the letters the other way
round, as $\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\}$, and because of that symmetry it is the same sum:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\} &= \sum_a\matter m_a\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,(\vect x_a)\\ &= \int\matter\rho\,\nabla h\cdot\nabla g\,\dd V .\end{aligned}} \label{eq:two-moments}$$

The paper calls this "the response of the first to an impulse generated by the second". Read with equation
[](#eq:generated), $\dot Q_g$ generates a motion that moves each part by $\lambda\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, and $Q_h$
responds at the rate $\{Q_h, \dot Q_g\}$. The tide is the mirror of this: $Q_g$ generates it, run backward, and
$\dot Q_h$ responds by $\epsilon$ times the same integral.[^bracket-unit] With $h = g$ the bracket is the second term
of equation [](/paper/#eq:push): the energy gained is $\epsilon\dot Q_g + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\{Q_g, \dot Q_g\}$.

For $h = g = x$ the bracket is $\{\matter Mx_{\rm cm}, P_x\} = \matter M$, the total mass. For
$h = g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$ it is $\int\matter\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\dd V$, the moment of inertia, which the steady field
outside cannot see (chapter 1.6). For the paper's steel ball of radius 10 cm and 32.9 kg it is
$\tfrac25 \times 32.9 \times 0.1^2 = 0.13\unit{kg\,m^2}$. Even for $h$ and $g$ harmonic, the product
$\nabla h\cdot\nabla g$ is in general not harmonic (here it is $x^2 + y^2$, whose Laplacian is 4), so the field at one
time does not hold this integral. The paper shows that for well-chosen pairs of harmonic $h$ and $g$ the products
$\nabla h\cdot\nabla g$ include every wave $e^{i\vect k\cdot\vect x}$, so these brackets give every Fourier component
of the density (chapter 13.2). Readings at two times hold what a reading at one time cannot.

The same right-hand side appears twice more in the paper; we state both. In quantum mechanics the commutator
$[A, B] = AB - BA$ of these two quantities is $i\hbar$ times their Poisson bracket, with
$\hbar = 1.05 \times 10^{-34}\unit{J\,s}$ the reduced Planck constant (chapter 8.2 builds commutators from brackets):

{{paper eq:hair-commutator}}

And in a warm body the thermal motion supplies the kicks: the average of $\dot Q_h\dot Q_g$ is $k_BT$ times the same
integral, with $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant and $T$ the temperature, which is the paper's equation
[](/paper/#eq:thermal-moment) (chapters 4.2 and 4.6).

::: check
### Q

Compute $\{x, p^2\}$ and $\{p, x^2\}$ for one particle moving along a line.

### A

By the product rule in the second slot, $\{x, p^2\} = 2p\{x, p\} = 2p$, and $\{p, x^2\} = 2x\{p, x\} = -2x$.

### Q

Which quantity generates turning about a $z$ axis through a fixed centre, and why is it a constant of motion for a
particle pulled toward that centre?

### A

The angular momentum $L_z$. A central potential depends only on the distance from the centre, which a turn about an
axis through the centre leaves unchanged, so $\{L_z, H\} = 0$, and by Noether's theorem $L_z$ is constant.

### Q

In equation [](/paper/#eq:push), which term vanishes for a body at rest, and why?

### A

The first, $\epsilon\dot Q_g = \epsilon\sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g(\vect x_a)$, because every momentum is zero.

### Q

A uniform ball of 1 kg and radius 0.1 m, at rest, is kicked by the tide $g = \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2)$ with
$\epsilon = 1\unit{s^{-1}}$. How much energy does it gain, and how much would a thin shell of the same mass and radius
gain?

### A

The ball gains $\tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac25\matter MR^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J}$ and the shell
$\tfrac12\epsilon^2 \times \tfrac23\matter MR^2 = 3.3 \times 10^{-3}\unit{J}$, in the ratio $3 : 5$.
:::

**The idea to keep: in phase space every quantity changes at the rate of its bracket with the energy, and every
quantity generates a motion of its own. A sudden tide is the motion generated by a moment of the mass, and the energy
it leaves, exactly $\epsilon\dot Q_g + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\int\matter\rho\,|\nabla g|^2\,\dd V$, weighs what the steady
pull cannot.**

::: problems
### Practice

Show from Hamilton's equations that the cart's motion is $q = A\cos\omega t$, $p = -\matter m\omega A\sin\omega t$, and
check that this point moves on the ellipse $H = E$ and that the ellipse's area is $2\pi E/\omega$.

::: solution
The equations give $\dot q = p/\matter m$ and $\dot p = -kq$. With $q = A\cos\omega t$, the first gives
$p = \matter m\dot q = -\matter m\omega A\sin\omega t$, and then $\dot p = -\matter m\omega^2A\cos\omega t$, which
equals $-kq$ when $\omega^2 = k/\matter m$. The energy is
$\tfrac12\matter m\omega^2A^2\sin^2\omega t + \tfrac12kA^2\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12kA^2$, the same at all times, so the
point stays on one ellipse, with half-axes $A$ and $\matter m\omega A$. Its area is $\pi\matter m\omega A^2 =
2\pi(\tfrac12kA^2)/\omega$, because $\matter m\omega^2 = k$.
:::

### Stretch

Show that the rates of change of two moments have the bracket
$\{\dot Q_h, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot[(\nabla\nabla h)\nabla g - (\nabla\nabla g)\nabla h]$, with both
Hessians and both gradients taken at $\vect x_a$, where $\nabla\nabla h$ is the Hessian of $h$ (chapter 1.4). With the
momentum per unit volume in place of the sum over parts, this is the paper's equation
[](/paper/#eq:flow-bracket); the paper's letter for the momentum per unit volume is $\vect J$, which in this chapter
is the impulse. Show that the bracket vanishes for $h = x$, $g = y$.

::: solution
Both quantities depend on positions and momenta. The derivative of $\dot Q_h = \sum_a\vect p_a\cdot\nabla h(\vect x_a)$
with respect to $\vect x_a$ is the vector $(\nabla\nabla h)\,\vect p_a$, and with respect to $\vect p_a$ it is $\nabla
h(\vect x_a)$. So $\{\dot Q_h, \dot Q_g\} = \sum_a[(\nabla\nabla h)\vect p_a\cdot\nabla g - \nabla h\cdot(\nabla\nabla
g)\vect p_a]$. A Hessian is symmetric, so this is $\sum_a\vect p_a\cdot[(\nabla\nabla h)\nabla g - (\nabla\nabla
g)\nabla h]$. For $h = x$ and $g = y$ both Hessians are zero, and so is the bracket.
:::

### The paper

The paper's [Appendix C](/paper/#app:proofs), *The moments and the weight*, proves that the gradients of harmonic
functions at the $N$ places of a body's parts can take any values. Use this to show that a quantity $F$ whose bracket
with every moment $Q_h$ vanishes does not depend on the momenta, and that if its bracket with $H$ also vanishes, it is
a constant.

::: solution
By the bracket [](#eq:bracket), $\{F, Q_h\} = -\sum_a\matter m_a\nabla h(\vect x_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$,
the form the paper writes. The vectors $\matter m_a\nabla h(\vect x_a)$ can be chosen at will, so this can vanish for
every $h$ only if every $\partial F/\partial\vect p_a$ is zero: $F$ depends on the places alone. Then
$\{F, H\} = \sum_a(\vect p_a/\matter m_a)\cdot\partial F/\partial\vect x_a$. This must vanish for every state, and the
momenta can take any values, so every $\partial F/\partial\vect x_a$ is zero and $F$ is a constant.
:::
