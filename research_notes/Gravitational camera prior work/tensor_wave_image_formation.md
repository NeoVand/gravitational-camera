## 1. Polarization of gravitational radiation and Stokes parameters (spin weights, harmonic expansions)

### Takeaway
A Stokes description of gravitational-wave polarization has existed since Anile & Breuer (1974) and is standard in stochastic-background work: I and V are ordinary (spin-0) functions on the sky, Q ± iU is spin ±4 (harmonics start at l = 4) and the strain amplitude h₊ ± ih× is spin ±2 (harmonics start at l = 2, with a grad/curl = E/B split). All of it is far-field, plane-wave-superposition formalism; none of the excerpts seen treats finite-distance sources or a two-point tensor coherency matrix.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** A. M. Anile & R. A. Breuer, "Gravitational Stokes parameters", Astrophys. J. 189 (1 April 1974). The NASA record says the paper defines electromagnetic and gravitational Stokes parameters within general relativity, derives a general-relativistic radiative transfer equation for polarized radiation, and generalizes the Stokes concept to metric theories of gravity with six independent polarization states instead of two. — [NASA NTRS record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19740047288). Page range 39–49 comes from a secondary reference list — [Springer chapter reference list](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-35789-6_4).
- **[V]** Romano & Cornish, "Detection methods for stochastic gravitational-wave backgrounds: a unified treatment", Living Rev. Relativ. 20 (2017), DOI 10.1007/s41114-017-0004-1, arXiv:1608.06889. Search excerpt: Q ± iU are spin-4 fields that can be written as linear combinations of spin-weight ±4 spherical harmonics, while I and V are ordinary scalars on the sphere; circular polarization V appears in the isotropic part of the background while linear polarization does not. — [arXiv:1608.06889](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.06889); bibliographic data from [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41114-017-0004-1).
- **[V]** A 2026 paper, "Polarized Anisotropic Stochastic Gravitational Wave Background Search with Ground-Based Detector Networks" (arXiv:2605.25772), states that spin ±4 harmonics exist only for l ≥ 4, so the E and B spectra of linear polarization begin at l = 4, and gives the E-mode combination `a^E = −(a⁺ + a⁻)/2`. — [arXiv:2605.25772](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25772).
- **[V]** N. Anil Kumar, M. Çalışkan, G. Sato-Polito, M. Kamionkowski, L. - **[V]** A search excerpt returned for the Kato & Soda query gives the basis-rotation law `Q′ − iU′ = e^{−4iψ}(Q − iU)`, i.e. spin weight 4 (the sign depends on the rotation convention). — [ar5iv:1512.09139](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1512.09139).
- **[V]** R. Kato & J. Soda, "Probing circular polarization in stochastic gravitational wave background with pulsar timing arrays", Phys. Rev. D 93, 062003 (2016), arXiv:1512.09139: computes generalized overlap reduction functions for a circularly polarized background, gives a method to separate intensity (I mode) and circular polarization (V mode), and finds circular polarization cannot be detected for an isotropic background. — [arXiv:1512.09139](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1512.09139).

- **[V]** Conneely, Jaffe & Mingarelli, "On the Amplitude and Stokes Parameters of a Stochastic Gravitational-Wave Background" (arXiv:1808.05920): compares two descriptions of a background, one based on the amplitudes of the polarization fields and one on Stokes parameters. — [arXiv:1808.05920](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.05920).
- **[V]** Gair, Romano, Taylor & Mingarelli, "Mapping gravitational-wave backgrounds using methods from CMB analysis: Application to pulsar timing arrays" (arXiv:1406.4664, posted 18 June 2014): any background can be decomposed as a sum of gradient and curl modes; the response of a pulsar timing array to curl modes is identically zero, so "half of the gravitational-wave sky will never be observed using pulsar timing, no matter how many pulsars are used"; an isotropic, unpolarised, uncorrelated background can be represented accurately with only three modes. — [arXiv:1406.4664](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1406.4664).

- **[V]** Other normalizations exist: one paper defines `P_RL = (Q + iU)/2` and expands it in spin-4 harmonics. — [arXiv:1610.05702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.05702).

### Inferences
- Relation to image formation: I(f, n) is the incoherent "intensity image"; V and Q ± iU are its polarization images; the grad/curl (E/B) amplitudes of h₊ ± ih× are the coherent complex-amplitude image. The spin-weight bookkeeping (2 for amplitude, 0 and 4 for second moments) is the tensor replacement for the scalar field and intensity of Fourier optics.
- The confirmed statements "spin ±4 ⇒ l ≥ 4" and "curl modes invisible to pulsar timing" are both instances of a detector-dependent null space in the harmonic domain, which is the kind of statement a camera theory must make for each pixel type.

## 2. GW radiometry and map-making: overlap reduction function as visibility, diffraction limit, van Cittert–Zernike analogue

### Takeaway
The radiometer / cross-correlation formalism is, in structure, a far-field van Cittert–Zernike relation for one scalar output per detector, and the literature uses radio-astronomy language (baseline, Earth-rotation aperture synthesis, point-spread function, diffraction limit Δθ ≈ c/(2fD)). One targeted search found no paper that states an explicit "van Cittert–Zernike theorem for gravitational waves" or a tensor coherence theory for gravitational-wave detector arrays.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** Mitra et al., "Gravitational wave radiometry: Mapping a stochastic gravitational wave background" (arXiv:0708.2728): a radiometer uses Earth's rotation to build a sky map from detector pairs as in radio astronomy; two detectors separated by a baseline are pointed at a chosen direction by adding a time delay between their data streams. — [arXiv:0708.2728](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0708.2728).
- **[V]** Earth's rotation changes detector orientations and baselines, which allows sky mapping with only two detectors. — [arXiv:1504.01714, "Fast Gravitational Wave Radiometry using Data Folding"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.01714).
- **[V]** A numerical point-spread-function study finds the spread of a point source depends strongly on gravitational-wave frequency, and also on sky position and detector orientation. — [arXiv:1112.3090](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1112.3090).
- **[V]** A paper titled "Aperture synthesis for gravitational-wave data analysis: Deterministic sources" exists as gr-qc/0010033 (Penn State; author and journal not shown in the excerpt). — [gr-qc/0010033](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0010033).
- **[V]** Thrane, Ballmer, Romano, Mitra, Talukder, Bose & Mandic, "Probing the anisotropies of a stochastic gravitational-wave background using a network of ground-based laser interferometers" (arXiv:0910.0858; article number 122002): maximum-likelihood estimation of the angular power distribution; the isotropic and radiometer searches are limiting cases; uses singular-value decomposition to regularize the deconvolution of the detector response. — [arXiv:0910.0858](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/0910.0858); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/probing-the-anisotropies-of-a-stochastic-gravitational-wave-backg/).
- **[V]** Romano & Cornish give the diffraction-limit estimate `Δθ ≃ λ/2D = c/(2fD)` (f = gravitational-wave frequency, D = separation of a detector pair) and define l_max as the largest l of a spherical-harmonic decomposition with angular features of size Δθ. — [arXiv:1608.06889](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.06889).
- **[V]** Floden, Mandic, Matas & Tsukada, "Angular resolution of the search for anisotropic stochastic gravitational-wave background with terrestrial gravitational-wave detectors", PRD 106, 023010 (arXiv:2203.17141): past searches chose l_max from a diffraction-limit argument; the paper argues the resolution limit actually comes from phase coherence between sources at different angular separations, that low-order modes are best for first detection, and that detected sources can be localized with higher-order modes better than the diffraction limit predicts. — [arXiv:2203.17141](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.17141). - **[V]** Renzini & Contaldi, "Mapping incoherent gravitational wave backgrounds", MNRAS 481(4), 4650–4661 (2018), DOI 10.1093/mnras/sty2546, arXiv:1806.11360: a pixel-based maximum-likelihood map-maker that takes cross-correlations of sets of generalised baselines as input, applied to a subset of Advanced LIGO data. — [arXiv:1806.11360](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.11360). ### Inferences
- Dictionary (mine, based on the recalled formula below): cross-spectrum of two detector outputs ↔ visibility; `u = fΔx/c` ↔ baseline in wavelengths; `P(f,Ω)` ↔ sky brightness; `Σ_A F₁^A F₂^A` ↔ product of primary beams (broad, quadrupolar, sign-changing); Fisher/"beam" matrix ↔ dirty beam; `P̂ = Γ⁻¹X` ↔ deconvolution. The overlap reduction function is the visibility of a uniform sky.
- What is missing relative to radio polarimetry: each interferometer delivers one scalar projection `D^ij h_ij`, so the existing formalism is a scalar-channel equation with fixed "feeds". An all-component point detector would deliver a 5-component (symmetric trace-free) vector per pixel and a 5×5 coherency matrix per baseline; that generalization was not seen.
- Floden et al.'s point (resolution set by phase coherence and signal-to-noise, not by c/(2fD) alone) is the gravitational-wave version of super-resolution of sparse scenes and should carry over to a camera.

## 3. Angular resolution and near-field (wavefront-curvature) effects of GW detector arrays

### Takeaway
Printed resolution formulas have the form δθ ~ (1/SNR)(λ/D), with D the pulsar distance (coherent pulsar timing), the detector baseline (ground networks) or the orbit (LISA). Near-field treatment exists only as first-order wavefront-curvature ("Fresnel") corrections to the pulsar term, used to measure source distance, not to form images.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** Boyle & Pen, "Pulsar timing arrays as imaging gravitational wave telescopes: angular resolution and source (de)confusion" (arXiv:1010.4337): with accurately known pulsar distances the angular precision is of order (1/SNR) × (gravitational wavelength / pulsar distance), each well-measured pulsar acting like a baseline of a radio interferometer; with poorly known distances a looser limit applies that scales with the square root of that ratio, the source being localized within the quadrilateral of quiet pulsars around it, and the array can still characterize up to `(2N/7)(1 − 1/(2F))` sources per frequency bin (N pulsars, F frequency bins). — [arXiv:1010.4337](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/1010.4337).
- **[V]** A 2026 review credits Tsai et al. with the coherent-regime diffraction expression `δθ ≈ (1/(S/N)) (λ_GW/D_array)`. — [arXiv:2603.13643, "Pulsar timing arrays: the emerging gravitational-wave landscape"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.13643).
- **[V]** A 2025 paper reports a fundamental resolution limit of about 58° when pulsar distances are unknown, reached with roughly 20 or more pulsars. — [arXiv:2507.21380](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21380).
- **[V]** Grunthal, Champion, Thrane, Nathan, Kramer & Miles, "Optimising gravitational-wave sky maps for pulsar timing arrays" (arXiv:2601.13957, Astron. Astrophys. 2026): derives a point-spread function for the angular resolution of a pulsar timing array; notes earlier methods cap resolution globally by limiting the number of spherical-harmonic modes to the number of pulsars, whereas local resolving power depends strongly on the pulsar sky distribution (citing Boyle & Pen). — [arXiv:2601.13957](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13957); [A&A](https://www.aanda.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202557896).
- **[V]** A 2026 title on the same theme (content not examined): "Gravitational-Wave Sky Mapping with Pulsar Timing Arrays: The Full Earth-Pulsar Response and Fundamental Resolution Limits". — [arXiv:2607.19329](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.19329).
- **[V]** Deng & Finn, "Pulsar timing array observations of gravitational wave source timing parallax", MNRAS 414(1), 50–58 (2011), arXiv:1008.0320: the standard response assumes plane wavefronts; for point sources closer than about 100 Mpc the response is sensitive to source parallax across the pulsar–Earth baseline. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/pulsar-timing-array-observations-of-gravitational-wave-source-tim/); [ar5iv:1008.0320](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1008.0320).
- **[V]** McGrath & Creighton, "Fresnel models for gravitational wave effects on pulsar timing", MNRAS 505, 4531–4554 (2021), arXiv:2011.09561: generalizes plane-wave timing models to a Fresnel regime that includes wavefront curvature; the models reduce to plane-wave ones for small Fresnel number (F → 0) or source–pulsar alignment (r̂·p̂ → ±1); curvature makes the wave reach the pulsar later than the plane-wave prediction; sources with chirp mass ~10⁹ M_⊙ and orbital frequency above 10 nHz are good candidates; source-distance uncertainty can be below 10% out to ~100 Mpc. — [arXiv:2011.09561](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09561).
- **[V]** D'Orazio & Loeb, "Using gravitational wave parallax to measure the Hubble parameter with pulsar timing arrays" (arXiv:2009.06084): comoving distance from the curvature of gravitational wavefronts across the Earth–pulsar baseline; with ~100 pulsars at 1–20 kpc and 1% distance errors, the Hubble constant is measurable to better than 30% from one source at 0.1 ≲ z ≲ 2. — [arXiv:2009.06084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06084v1).
- **[V]** McGrath, D'Orazio & Creighton (arXiv:2208.06495): "Fresnel" models are those keeping first-order curvature terms; the parallax distance is hard to recover because of the pulsar-distance wrapping problem, which requires sub-parsec pulsar distance precision. — [arXiv:2208.06495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06495v1).
- **[V]** Cutler, "Angular resolution of the LISA gravitational wave detector", Phys. Rev. D 57, 7089–7102 (1998), gr-qc/9703068: angular information comes from the detector's motion around the Sun and from modulation by its time-varying orientation; results are solid-angle errors ΔΩ_S for monochromatic sources and massive-black-hole mergers at S/N = 10 over one year; the resolution "is not a fixed quantity" but depends on signal type and on what else must be estimated. — [gr-qc/9703068](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9703068); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevd.57.7089).
- **[V]** Wen & Chen, "Geometrical expression for the angular resolution of a network of gravitational-wave detectors", Phys. Rev. D 81, 082001 (2010), arXiv:1003.2504: resolution depends on the areas formed by projections of detector pairs, weighted by detector sensitivities. — [arXiv:1003.2504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1003.2504). Schutz's network paper adds that triangulation errors are proportional to arrival-time error divided by the baseline light-travel time. — [arXiv:1102.5421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1102.5421).

### Inferences
- All confirmed formulas are parameter-estimation (Fisher-matrix) statements for a few point sources in the radiating far zone, with at most a first-order Fresnel phase. They transfer to a camera as "localization precision ≈ diffraction width / SNR".
- Geometry (mine): for an element at distance L from the array centre in direction p and a source at distance R in direction n, `|Rn − Lp| ≈ R − L cosθ + L² sin²θ/(2R)`, so the curvature phase is `π sin²θ · L²/(λR)`. Focusing in range needs `L²/(λR) ≳ 1`.
- For everyday scenes at audio frequencies the wavelength (c/f ≈ 300 km at 1 kHz) far exceeds the scene and the array, so the camera works in the reactive near zone (r ≪ λ), where the field is quasi-static tidal gravity. None of the confirmed gravitational-wave array literature covers that regime as an imaging problem.

### Gaps
- Exact prefactors of the Boyle & Pen limits and the explicit Cutler and Wen–Chen formulas were not visible in excerpts.
- No search was run for near-zone (r ≲ λ) tensor-field imaging or gravity-gradient tomography with coherent arrays.

  - McGrath & Creighton Fresnel number: `F ≡ L²/(λ_gw R)` (L = Earth–pulsar distance, R = Earth–source distance).
  - Fairhurst, New J. Phys. 11, 123006 (2009): timing accuracy `σ_t = 1/(2πρσ_f)` (ρ = SNR, σ_f = effective bandwidth), giving localization of order `c σ_t/D` per baseline D.

## 4. Single-point direction finding and local field invariants (Bel–Robinson, E_ij and B_ij, vector-sensor analogue, angular band-limit of a point detector)

### Takeaway
The local invariants exist (super-energy density and super-Poynting vector from the gravito-electric and gravito-magnetic tidal tensors), and single-detector direction finding from the electric-type tidal tensor alone exists (spherical antennas, SOGRO: direction "modulo angle π"). The electromagnetic template exists (Nehorai–Paldi vector sensor with a Poynting-vector estimator). One search found no proposal to use E_ij and B_ij together as a "gravitational vector sensor", and the only band-limit statements found are the low-frequency interferometer/LISA ones.
