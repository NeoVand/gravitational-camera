## 1. Thermal graviton emission from hot plasma and stars (Weinberg, Gould, Galtsov–Grats, Steane, García-Cely & Ringwald)

### Takeaway
The Sun's thermal gravitational-wave luminosity is consistently put at roughly 10^8 W across sixty years of calculations: about 6×10^7 W (Weinberg 1965, as quoted by later authors), 7.9×10^14 erg/s = 7.9×10^7 W (Gould 1985) and 1.3×10^8 W (García-Cely & Ringwald 2024/2025), carried mostly by keV gravitons from electron–ion and electron–electron bremsstrahlung plus photoproduction, with a hydrodynamic-fluctuation term (shear-viscosity controlled) completing the low-frequency end. This is about 3×10^-19 of the Sun's photon luminosity, and corresponds to roughly 10^24 gravitons/s, i.e. about 4×10^-4 gravitons cm^-2 s^-1 at Earth (Dyson).

## 2. Fluctuation–dissipation / hydrodynamic formulation: shear viscosity controls long-wavelength thermal GW production; the cosmic gravitational microwave background; relic graviton temperature

### Takeaway
It is established (Ghiglieri & Laine 2015; Ghiglieri, Jackson, Laine & Zhu 2020) that a thermal plasma emits gravitational waves at a rate fixed by the transverse-traceless stress correlator, which at the longest wavelengths reduces to the shear viscosity; the same physics appears on the absorption side as Hawking's damping rate 16πGη. Applied to the early universe this gives the "cosmic gravitational microwave background" peaking at about 80 GHz with an amplitude proportional to the maximum temperature of the hot big bang; the equilibrium-relic alternative (a ~0.9 K graviton blackbody from Planck-era decoupling) is standard lore but is erased by inflation.

### Cited Findings

Abstract-level result: "for the largest wavelengths the emission rate is proportional to the shear viscosity of the plasma." Above the electroweak crossover (T > 160 GeV) the viscosity is dominated by the most weakly interacting species (right-handed leptons); the authors note the viscosity estimate (their eq. (4.2)) is the most "optimistic". **[P]** — [arXiv:1504.02569](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.02569); [JCAP](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1475-7516/2015/07/022)

### Inferences
- **Explicit production-rate formula (researcher derivation; matches the researcher's recollection of Ghiglieri–Laine but was NOT confirmed against the paper text).** Hawking's energy-damping rate Γ = 16πGη plus detailed balance, df/dt = Γ[n_B(k) − f], with e_GW = 2∫d³k/(2π)³ k f, gives (ħ = c = k_B = 1, G = 1/m_Pl²):

- **Standard-lore relic graviton temperature:** T_g = T_γ (3.91/106.75)^{1/3} = 2.725 K × 0.332 ≈ 0.905 K (researcher arithmetic; no retrieved source states "0.9 K" explicitly, and arXiv:2412.11282 quotes ≲ 0.61 K with the same g_* inputs — an unresolved discrepancy). - Caveats on the 0.9 K lore, from the cited findings: it requires gravitons to have been in equilibrium at the Planck epoch, only Standard Model degrees of freedom (g_* = 106.75), and no inflation afterwards.

## 3. CRUCIAL novelty check: Kirchhoff's law (emissivity = absorption × Planck function, absorption set by shear viscosity) applied to ordinary condensed matter

### Takeaway
Within the searches that could be run, **no paper was found that applies a gravitational Kirchhoff law / fluctuation–dissipation emissivity to ordinary condensed matter** (liquids, solids, the Earth's interior, planets, laboratory bodies). The ingredients all exist separately — Hawking's viscous absorption rate, the Ghiglieri–Laine viscosity formula for relativistic plasmas, and García-Cely & Ringwald's hydrodynamic-fluctuation term for the solar plasma — and thermal-GW estimates exist for the Sun, white dwarfs, neutron stars, photon gases and the CMB, but all of those found use bremsstrahlung or field-theoretic methods, not viscosity-based Kirchhoff emission, and none addresses room-temperature matter. This is a negative result from a limited search and should be stated as such.

### Inferences
- **The gap appears real but is not proven.** The generalisation is a one-line consequence of known results, so it may exist as a remark inside a paper not surfaced here (most plausibly in García-Cely & Ringwald's hydrodynamic section, in Weinberg §10.8-type textbook discussions of graviton absorption, or in the Simidzija et al. paper). The defensible claim for the project is: "to our knowledge, the viscosity/Kirchhoff emissivity has been applied to relativistic plasmas (Ghiglieri & Laine) and to the solar plasma (García-Cely & Ringwald) but not to condensed matter at ordinary temperatures."
- **What the Kirchhoff construction gives (researcher derivation, see §2):** absorption coefficient α = 16πGη/c³ (per metre); volume emissivity ε_ω = 4π α B_ω(T) = 16Gηħω³/[πc⁵(e^{ħω/kT} − 1)]; total P/V = 4ασT⁴ if η were frequency-independent.
- **Illustrative number (researcher arithmetic, not literature):** water, η = 1.0×10^-3 Pa·s, T = 300 K → α ≈ 1.2×10^-37 m^-1 (absorption length ≈ 8×10^36 m); P/V ≈ 2.3×10^-34 W m^-3; at a mean graviton energy 2.7 kT ≈ 1.1×10^-20 J this is ≈ 2×10^-14 gravitons s^-1 m^-3, i.e. about one thermal graviton per cubic metre per 1.5 million years. This is an upper-end estimate, because the zero-frequency viscosity does not apply at thermal frequencies (see next bullet).
- **The physics that makes condensed matter non-trivial (and hence a genuine research contribution):** the relevant quantity is the frequency-dependent shear response, Im G_R^{xy,xy}(ω) = ω η(ω), at ω up to ~kT/ħ ≈ 4×10^13 rad/s (300 K). For liquids η(ω) falls off above the Maxwell relaxation rate (τ_M = η/G_∞ ~ ps); for solids the static viscosity is irrelevant and the absorption is set by the lossy part of the shear modulus (internal friction, μ″ = μ/Q, effective η(ω) = μ/(ωQ)). The Earth's mantle viscosity (~10^21 Pa·s) applies only below ~1/τ_M ~ 10^-10 Hz and cannot be inserted at GHz–THz frequencies. The same crossover (ω² rise below the collision rate, flat bremsstrahlung plateau above) is what García-Cely & Ringwald's "hydrodynamic completion" encodes for the Sun.
- Because α·L ≪ 1 for any body (α ≈ 1.2×10^-34 m^-1 per Pa·s of viscosity, so even a stellar radius of ~10^9 m gives a negligible optical depth), every object is optically thin to gravitons: emission is volume emission, never blackbody surface emission, and there is no self-absorption. Kirchhoff's law is then simply "power = 4π α B_ω × volume".

## 4. Graviton emission by atoms, molecules and nuclei

### Takeaway
The hydrogen 3d→1s single-graviton decay rate is the canonical number, but the literature disagrees by five orders of magnitude: Weinberg's value 2.5×10^-44 s^-1 (lifetime 4×10^43 s), reproduced by Rothman & Boughn, versus Kiefer's 5.7×10^-40 s^-1 (5.6×10^31 yr) and a 2026 value 2.85×10^-39 s^-1. Boughn & Rothman (2006) explicitly attribute errors in the earlier literature to a gauge-invariance subtlety of the linearized interaction Hamiltonian. Molecular and nuclear graviton emission was treated by Halpern & Laurent (1964); no modern numbers for molecular rotational/vibrational graviton rates were retrieved.

### Inferences
- **Arithmetic cross-check (researcher):** G m_e³ c α⁶/ħ² = 2.05×10^-37 s^-1. - Scaling for the graviton budget: Γ_grav/Γ_photon ~ 2.5×10^-44/10^9 ~ 10^-53 for an optical-frequency atomic transition, so atomic line emission is negligible compared with collisional/thermal continuum emission in any ordinary scene; the rate scales as G m³α⁶/ħ² ∝ G (quadrupole)² ω⁵/(ħc⁵).
- Selection rules follow from the spin-2, quadrupole (E2-like) nature of the coupling: ΔL = 2 (e.g. d→s), no dipole graviton emission (general textbook reasoning; no source retrieved in this session stating the rules verbatim).

## 5. Laboratory generation of gravitational waves

### Takeaway
Every proposed laboratory source radiates absurdly little: the textbook rotating steel beam (20 m, 490 t, 28 rad/s) gives about 2×10^-29 W; a Romero–Dehnen array of 10^4 piezoelectric crystals about 10^-29 W; petawatt lasers give strains ~10^-37 at 10 m; the only proposal claiming a non-negligible power (>1 erg/s = 10^-7 W) uses nuclear explosions. Electromagnetic schemes (Gertsenshtein conversion, Grishchuk–Sazhin cavities, "gravitational Hertz experiments") exist as proposals only; none has been realized.

## 6. Electromagnetic fields as gravitational-wave sources

### Takeaway
The gravitational field of light has been treated since Tolman, Ehrenfest & Podolsky (1931, thin "pencil of light") and in modern linearized-gravity form by Rätzel, Wilkens & Menzel (2016, finite laser pulse: field modulated with |E| for linear polarization, effects localized at emission and absorption); GW emission from standing electromagnetic waves in a cavity goes back to Grishchuk & Sazhin (1974). No radiated-power numbers for cavities or beams were retrieved.

### Inferences
- The Rätzel result that linear polarization imprints a modulation at the optical field's |E| frequency means a linearly polarized beam is, in principle, a source of GWs at twice the optical frequency — the EM analogue of a quadrupole oscillator — while a circularly polarized beam is not. This is the cleanest "everyday" coherent source at optical GW frequencies, though with amplitudes far below any thermal background estimate.
- Thermal light in a scene (blackbody photons at 300 K or sunlight) is itself a graviton source via photon–photon and photon–matter stress fluctuations; by the T^7-type scaling of Simidzija et al. and the 10^-66 erg s^-1 cm^-3 K-scale prefactor of the 1988 photon-bath result, it is negligible next to matter emission at room temperature. (Qualitative inference; the two formulas were not retrieved in usable form.)

## 7. High-frequency GW detection and its limits; single-graviton detection

### Takeaway
The Living Reviews white paper (2021; updated 2025 as "...above 10 kHz") frames the >10 kHz band as nearly free of astrophysical sources and therefore a window on new physics, with proposed detector sensitivities still many orders of magnitude from any thermal or solar signal (e.g. IAXO-type conversion detectors reach h_c ~ 10^-29 at 10^18 Hz, ~15 orders above the Sun). On single gravitons: Dyson and Rothman–Boughn argue that detection is possible in principle but hopeless in practice for thermal sources; Tobar et al. argue that stimulated single-graviton absorption from astrophysical GWs is observable in ground-state acoustic resonators; Carney, Domcke & Rodd agree that such clicks are achievable but show they do not prove quantization.

### Inferences
- The detection literature splits by regime in a way that matters for the Gravitational Camera. Stimulated absorption from a highly occupied coherent classical wave (Tobar et al.; Carney et al.) is feasible because the mode occupation is enormous (~10^36 gravitons per LIGO event). The Camera's sources are the opposite regime: thermal, incoherent, with occupation numbers ≪ 1 per mode at the detector, which is exactly Dyson's and Rothman–Boughn's "possible in principle, impossible in practice" case (Planck-area atomic cross sections, neutrino backgrounds ≥ 10^34 times the signal).
- Gap between best projected high-frequency sensitivity and the brightest local thermal source: ~15 orders of magnitude in h_c at 10^18 Hz (IAXO vs Sun), i.e. ~30 orders in power. Room-temperature scene emission (§3 inference) is tens of orders weaker still. "Idealized, arbitrarily sensitive pixels" is therefore a load-bearing assumption of the project, and the photon-counting (shot-noise) limit — a few solar gravitons per m² per second in total, ~10 per cm² per day in the optical band — is the real constraint on image formation even with perfect detectors.
